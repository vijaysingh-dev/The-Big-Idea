# How HLD Interviews Work

> **Revision tags:** #interview #hld-process  
> **Related:** [[07.2 - Common Mistakes]] | [[07.3 - Communicating Tradeoffs]]

---

## What Is Being Evaluated

HLD interviews are not testing whether you know the "right" answer. There is no single right architecture for any problem. Interviewers are evaluating:

| Dimension | What They Look For |
|---|---|
| **Scope management** | Can you clarify requirements before designing? Do you know what's in vs out of scope? |
| **Estimation** | Can you estimate scale and derive architecture implications? |
| **Breadth** | Do you know the major building blocks and when to use them? |
| **Depth** | Can you go deep on 2–3 components when asked? |
| **Tradeoff reasoning** | Do you justify decisions or just state them? |
| **Failure thinking** | Do you consider what breaks, not just what works? |
| **Communication** | Can you explain your design clearly while drawing/talking? |
| **Seniority signal** | Do you sound like someone who has operated systems, not just read about them? |

---

## Interview Format

Most HLD interviews are 45–60 minutes. The format:

```
0–5 min:   Clarify requirements (your job — don't wait to be asked)
5–10 min:  Estimation and assumptions
10–15 min: High-level architecture (the "boxes and arrows" diagram)
15–35 min: Deep dive on 2–3 key components (interviewer often drives this)
35–45 min: Failure handling, scaling discussion, tradeoffs
45–60 min: Wrap-up, questions
```

Interviewers at senior+ levels will push you to go deeper, challenge your choices, and ask "what if scale increases 10×?" Know that this is normal and expected — it's not a sign you're doing badly.

## What Good Looks Like (Level by Level)

### Mid-Level Engineer

- Clarifies functional requirements
- Draws a reasonable architecture diagram
- Identifies the obvious building blocks (load balancer, cache, DB)
- Gives some rationale for choices
- Handles happy path well; basic failure handling

### Senior Engineer

- Clarifies both functional and non-functional requirements
- Does back-of-envelope estimation and derives implications
- Justifies every major choice with a tradeoff
- Proactively addresses failure modes
- Can go deep on any component when asked
- Considers operational concerns (monitoring, deploys, migrations)

### Staff Engineer

- All of the above, plus:
- Identifies the hardest problems in the system proactively
- Proposes multiple approaches with explicit evaluation
- Thinks about team structure and ownership boundaries
- Considers cost, compliance, and long-term evolvability
- Pushes back on requirements if they lead to over-engineering

## The Evaluation Rubric (What Interviewers Actually Score)

Most companies score on a rubric with these dimensions:

**1. Problem Scoping (20%)**
- Did you clarify before designing?
- Did you make reasonable assumptions and state them?
- Did you define what's in scope?

**2. High-Level Design (25%)**
- Does the overall architecture make sense?
- Are the components reasonable for the stated requirements?
- Is the data flow clear?

**3. Technical Depth (25%)**
- When asked to go deep, can you?
- Do you know the internals of what you're proposing?
- Can you explain why a specific design choice is correct?

**4. Tradeoffs and Alternatives (20%)**
- Do you acknowledge limitations of your choices?
- Can you compare alternatives?
- Do you justify decisions based on requirements?

**5. Non-Functional Concerns (10%)**
- Failure handling
- Scalability
- Monitoring and operations

## How to Drive the Interview

You should drive the interview, not react to it. The interviewer should mostly be listening and asking occasional questions.

**The flow you control:**
```
"Let me start by clarifying what we're building."
  [ask 3–5 targeted questions]

"Based on your answers, let me state my assumptions."
  [write them down visibly]

"Let me estimate the scale so I know what we're designing for."
  [do 2-3 key calculations]

"Here's the high-level architecture I'm thinking about."
  [draw the boxes and arrows]

"The most interesting part of this problem is [X]. Let me go deep there first."
  [choose the hardest/most interesting component and deep-dive]

"Now let me address failure modes."
  [go through your design and identify what breaks]
```

If the interviewer steers you somewhere, follow them — they're telling you what they care about.

## The "Design Twitter" Problem

Interviewers often give vague prompts like "Design Twitter" or "Design Instagram." This is intentional. They're testing whether you know to scope.

**Never start drawing immediately.**

```
You: "Before I design anything, can I ask a few questions?"
     "Are we focused on the feed, direct messages, or the full platform?"
     "What's the expected scale — are we designing for Twitter's current scale or a new startup?"
     "Is there a specific non-functional requirement I should optimize for — latency, availability, consistency?"

Interviewer: "Focus on the feed. Design for 100M users. Optimize for feed read latency."

You: "Perfect. So I'll scope this to: timeline generation, post creation, and feed reads.
     I'll set aside DMs, trends, ads, and notifications as out of scope unless you want me to cover them."
```

This takes 3 minutes. It prevents you from spending 20 minutes designing the wrong thing.

## Using the Whiteboard / Shared Doc Effectively

- Start with the client and draw left to right (client → internet → backend → storage)
- Label every box clearly. Don't make the interviewer guess what "B" means
- Draw data flow arrows and label them (what's being sent)
- Use a consistent notation: rectangles for services, cylinders for databases, parallelograms for queues
- Don't erase — if you change something, cross it out and redraw. Shows how your thinking evolves

```
[Client] ──HTTP──→ [Load Balancer] ──→ [API Gateway] ──→ [Feed Service]
                                                              │
                                                    ┌─────────┤
                                                    ▼         ▼
                                               [Redis]   [PostgreSQL]
                                               (cache)    (DB)
```

## Interview Anti-Patterns

**Jumping to code:** You're being evaluated on system design, not implementation. If you find yourself writing code, stop.

**Designing in silence:** Think out loud. Even wrong thinking that's audible is better than correct thinking that's invisible to the interviewer.

**Not committing to a design:** Endlessly listing pros and cons without making a decision. At some point you must pick. "I'll go with X because Y."

**Over-abstracting:** "We'll use a distributed system with appropriate caching." Say what you actually mean. Interviewers can't evaluate vague statements.

**Ignoring failure:** You can't get a senior offer if you don't address failure modes. Every real system fails.

**Not checking in:** Designing in a corner for 20 minutes and then revealing it. Check in every 5–7 minutes: "Does this direction make sense? Should I go deeper anywhere?"
