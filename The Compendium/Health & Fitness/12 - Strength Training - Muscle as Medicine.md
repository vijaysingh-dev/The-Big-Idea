# Strength Training: Muscle as Medicine

*Health & Fitness · 2026-10-30*

Most Indian parents think of exercise as "walking" and of strength training as something for young men in gyms. That's a costly misunderstanding. After 40, muscle is one of the strongest predictors of how well and how long you live: it soaks up blood sugar, protects joints, prevents the falls that end independence, and it can be built at any age with no equipment at all.

---

## 1. Muscle is a metabolic organ, not just for lifting

[[01 - Human Body & Nutrition Basics]] described how training breaks down muscle fibres and protein rebuilds them stronger, and [[09 - Muscles, Bones & Joints - How the Frame Works]] covered how muscle is built. That's the structural side. The bigger story is what muscle does for the rest of the body:

- **It's the main destination for blood sugar.** After a meal, skeletal muscle takes up most of the glucose that insulin clears from the blood ([[04 - Hormones - The Body's Messaging System]]). More muscle means more storage space. Working muscle can also pull glucose in **without needing as much insulin**, which is why exercise directly lowers blood sugar even in people with insulin resistance ([[18 - Blood Sugar, Insulin Resistance & Diabetes]]).
- **It's a protein reserve for illness.** During a serious illness, injury or hospital stay, the body breaks down muscle for fuel and repair. People who start with more muscle recover better.
- **It protects joints.** Strong thigh and hip muscles absorb load that would otherwise go through the knee joint.
- **It keeps bones strong.** Muscles pulling on bones is the signal that tells bones to stay dense.
- **It prevents falls.** Leg strength and balance are what stop a trip from becoming a fall. In older adults, a fall that breaks a hip is often the beginning of the end of independent life.
- **It releases helpful signals.** Working muscle releases signalling molecules called **myokines** that reduce inflammation and improve metabolism elsewhere.

Studies consistently link greater muscle strength (even something as simple as grip strength) with lower risk of death from all causes, independently of how much cardio a person does.

## 2. Sarcopenia: the slow leak

**Sarcopenia** is the age-related loss of muscle mass and strength. It starts quietly from the 30s and speeds up after 50–60, especially with inactivity, low protein, illness or bed rest. A week in a hospital bed can cost an older person a meaningful share of their leg strength.

The danger is that it's invisible at first. Body weight may not change, because fat replaces the lost muscle. The result is the **thin-fat** body type common in Indians ([[18 - Blood Sugar, Insulin Resistance & Diabetes]]): more fat, less muscle, worse blood sugar, at the same weight.

Signs worth noticing:
- Needing hands to push up from a chair
- Difficulty climbing stairs or carrying a full bucket
- Slower walking speed
- Losing balance more easily

**Daily chores help, but often aren't enough.** Cleaning, farm work and walking keep people mobile. But muscle is maintained by working against a load that feels genuinely challenging, and most chores are repetitive and moderate. Adding a small amount of deliberate strength work fills the gap.

## 3. How muscle actually gets stronger

Three principles:

1. **Challenge.** Muscle adapts only when asked to do something harder than usual. The last few repetitions of a set should feel hard but still doable with good form, leaving about 2–3 repetitions "in the tank." Easy, endless repetitions mostly build endurance, not strength.
2. **Progressive overload.** As it gets easier, make it slightly harder: more repetitions, a slower tempo, a heavier object, a lower chair, an extra set. This gradual increase is what drives continued improvement.
3. **Recovery and protein.** Muscle is rebuilt in the hours and days after training, not during it. That needs sleep ([[14 - Sleep & Circadian Rhythm]]) and enough protein ([[11 - The Indian Plate - Rebuilding the Thali]]). Older adults especially benefit from a good protein portion at each meal.

## 4. How much is enough

WHO recommends that adults do **muscle-strengthening activities on 2 or more days a week**, working all major muscle groups, in addition to 150–300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity ([[13 - Cardio, Mobility & Balance]]).

A practical starting dose:
- **2–3 sessions a week**, on non-consecutive days
- **20–30 minutes** per session
- **5–6 exercises** covering legs, hips, back, chest and core
- **1–3 sets** of each, **8–15 repetitions** per set

Most of the benefit comes from going from nothing to something. Two short sessions a week is a large improvement over zero.

## 5. A no-equipment home routine for older adults

Everything below can be done at home with a chair, a wall, a step and household objects. Start easy, focus on control, and progress slowly.

1. **Sit-to-stand (chair squat).** Sit on a firm chair without armrests, arms crossed over the chest. Stand up fully, then sit back down slowly, taking 2–3 seconds to lower. *This is the single most important exercise for older adults*: it trains exactly the movement needed for daily independence.
   - *Easier:* use a higher chair or push with your hands.
   - *Harder:* use a lower seat, or hold a water bottle or small bag of atta.
2. **Wall push-up.** Hands on a wall at shoulder height, step back, lower your chest toward the wall and push back.
   - *Harder:* hands on a kitchen counter, then a sturdy table.
3. **Step-up.** Step onto the bottom stair (holding the rail) with one foot, bring the other up, step down. Alternate legs.
4. **Glute bridge.** Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat. Lift your hips until your body forms a straight line from shoulders to knees, hold for a second, lower.
5. **Row.** Pull a resistance band anchored at a door handle, or pull a bag held in one hand toward your waist while leaning on a table with the other hand.
6. **Farmer's carry.** Carry two equal loads (filled buckets, bags of grain, water bottles) and walk for 20–40 metres with an upright posture. Farming families do this naturally, but deliberately and with good posture.
7. **Calf raise.** Holding a wall, rise onto your toes, then lower slowly. Helps balance and walking.
8. **Balance:** stand on one leg near a wall or counter for up to 30 seconds per side. Progress by looking away or standing on a cushion.

A **resistance band** costs a few hundred rupees and adds a great deal of variety. Dumbbells or a gym add more, but they're not needed to start.

### A sample week
- **Monday, Wednesday, Friday:** sit-to-stand, wall push-up, step-up, glute bridge, row, calf raise. 2 sets each.
- **Every day:** walking, and the balance exercise while brushing teeth or waiting for chai.

## 6. Knees and strength

Knee pain in older Indian adults, especially women, is very often **osteoarthritis** (wear and inflammation of the knee joint's cartilage and surrounding tissue). [[21 - Joints & Bones - Osteoarthritis, Osteoporosis & Falls]] covers it in detail. The key point for this note: **exercise is a first-line treatment for knee osteoarthritis** in international guidelines, ahead of pain medicines. Strengthening the **quadriceps** (front thigh muscles) and hip muscles reduces pain and improves function, often as much as painkillers, without the side effects.

Practical tips for painful knees:
- **Mild discomfort (up to about 3–4 out of 10) during exercise is generally acceptable** if it settles within a day. Sharp pain, or pain that's worse the next day, means the exercise needs to be made easier.
- **Start with a high chair** for sit-to-stands and partial range of motion.
- **Knee-friendly options:** straight-leg raises lying down, seated knee extensions (straightening the leg while seated), glute bridges.
- **A physiotherapist** can assess the knee and set the right starting point. A few sessions to learn a home programme are often worth more than months of painkillers.
- **Losing weight**, if overweight, lowers the load on the knees considerably.

## 7. Safety: who should check with a doctor first

Strength training is safe for most people, including older adults and people with diabetes, when started gradually. **See a doctor before starting** if any of these apply:

- **Known heart disease**, a recent heart attack, stent or bypass, or chest pain on exertion. Cardiac rehabilitation programmes are designed exactly for this.
- **Uncontrolled high blood pressure.**
- **Recent surgery**, a hernia, or eye surgery.
- **Diabetes with eye or nerve damage**, or on insulin (for hypoglycaemia risk).
- **Osteoporosis** with past fractures.

**During exercise:**
- **Breathe out on the effort** and don't hold your breath. Holding your breath while straining spikes blood pressure.
- **Stop immediately** with chest pain or pressure, unusual breathlessness, dizziness, or palpitations, and seek care.
- **Start with less** than you think you can do.

## 8. Strength for every age

- **Children and teenagers:** active play, sports, climbing and bodyweight exercises build muscle and bones. Supervised strength training is safe for children; the old myth that it "stunts growth" is false.
- **Young adults:** the best time to build a reserve of muscle and bone that will be drawn down later in life.
- **Women:** strength training is especially valuable for bone density around and after menopause, when bone loss speeds up. It won't make women bulky.
- **Older adults:** the priority is keeping independence: getting up from the floor or a chair, climbing stairs, carrying things, and not falling.

## 9. Tracking progress

Simple tests to repeat every 2–3 months:
- **30-second chair stand:** how many full sit-to-stands in 30 seconds without using hands.
- **Grip strength:** a cheap hand dynamometer, or noticing whether opening jars and carrying bags gets easier.
- **Walking speed and stairs:** time to walk a fixed distance, or to climb one flight.
- **Waist size:** should fall slowly as muscle replaces fat.

---

## The layer, in one line

Muscle is the body's glucose sink, joint protector and fall insurance; it's lost silently with age and rebuilt by challenging it two or three times a week, with enough protein and sleep, using nothing more than a chair, a wall and a bucket.
