# Medicines Without Myths

*Health & Fitness · 2026-10-30*

In Indian households, two opposite mistakes happen at once: people fear long-term medicines that save lives ("once you start BP tablets you're stuck for life," "statins damage the liver") while freely taking pills that cause harm (antibiotics for every cold, painkillers for every ache, acid tablets daily for years). Getting medicines right is one of the highest-leverage things a family can do.

> This note explains how common medicines work and common misconceptions. Always follow your own doctor's advice about starting, changing or stopping a medicine.

---

## 1. Lifestyle and medicines are partners, not rivals

The earlier notes put lifestyle first, and for good reason: food, movement, sleep and no tobacco shift almost every risk factor at once. But lifestyle has limits:
- **Genes** can't be outrun — high Lp(a), familial high cholesterol, type 1 diabetes, autoimmune thyroid disease.
- **Ageing** stiffens arteries and wears out the pancreas regardless of effort.
- **Damage already done** — blocked arteries after a heart attack, scarred kidneys — needs active protection.
- **Time:** when BP is 170/100 or HbA1c is 10%, months of trying lifestyle alone means months of ongoing damage.

The right question isn't "medicine or lifestyle?" but "what combination gets this number to a safe target fastest and keeps it there?" Good lifestyle often means **lower doses and fewer medicines**, and sometimes medicines can be reduced later — but only with a doctor, based on readings.

## 2. Statins (for cholesterol)

**What they do:** statins (atorvastatin, rosuvastatin and others) reduce the liver's cholesterol production, so the liver pulls more LDL out of the blood. They lower LDL by roughly 30–50% or more depending on dose, and they **stabilise plaques**, making them less likely to rupture ([[19 - How Heart Disease Actually Happens]]). Large trials involving hundreds of thousands of people show they reduce heart attacks, strokes and deaths, especially in people at higher risk.

**Myths and facts:**
- *"Statins damage the liver."* A mild rise in liver enzymes occurs in a small percentage of users and usually settles; serious liver injury is very rare. Statins are often safe even in fatty liver, where heart risk is high.
- *"Statins cause diabetes."* They slightly raise the chance of developing diabetes in people already close to it, but the reduction in heart attacks and strokes is much larger than that risk.
- *"Statins cause muscle pain in everyone."* Muscle aches are commonly reported, but in blinded trials most of the aches occurred as often with placebo (dummy pills) as with statins — a "nocebo" effect, where expecting side effects causes them. Genuine statin muscle problems do exist; if they occur, switching statin, lowering the dose or alternate-day dosing usually helps. Serious muscle damage is rare.
- *"Once you start, you can never stop."* Statins keep working only while taken, the same way spectacles help only while worn. Stopping removes the benefit; that's not addiction.
- *"My cholesterol is normal now, so I can stop."* It's normal *because* of the statin.

**Other cholesterol medicines:** **ezetimibe** (reduces cholesterol absorption from the gut, often added to a statin), and **PCSK9 inhibitors** or **inclisiran** (injectables that lower LDL sharply, and modestly lower Lp(a), used in high-risk people). Newer Lp(a)-specific drugs are in trials.

## 3. Blood pressure medicines

Covered in [[20 - Blood Pressure, Stroke & Kidney Disease]]. The main myths:
- *"BP medicines are addictive."* They aren't. If BP goes back up after stopping, it's because the underlying high BP is still there.
- *"I'll take it only when I feel BP is high."* BP rarely causes symptoms. Taking it only on "bad days" leaves arteries exposed the rest of the time. Take it **every day** at the same time.
- *"They damage the kidneys."* Uncontrolled BP damages the kidneys. ACE inhibitors and ARBs actually protect kidneys in diabetes, though kidney function and potassium are checked after starting them.
- *"I feel dizzy, so I'll stop."* Dizziness on standing can mean the dose is too strong or the person is dehydrated; tell the doctor rather than stopping suddenly. Some medicines (such as beta-blockers and clonidine) should never be stopped abruptly.

## 4. Diabetes medicines

Covered in [[18 - Blood Sugar, Insulin Resistance & Diabetes]].
- **Metformin:** usually the first medicine. Lowers the liver's glucose output and improves insulin sensitivity; doesn't cause low sugar on its own; cheap and used for over 60 years. Stomach upset is common at first and reduced by taking it with food, starting low, or using an extended-release form. Long-term use can lower **vitamin B12**, so B12 should be checked periodically ([[22 - Thyroid, Anaemia & Common Deficiencies]]).
- **Sulfonylureas** (glimepiride, gliclazide): make the pancreas release more insulin; can cause low blood sugar, especially if meals are skipped ([[25 - Fast Killers - Emergencies & Warning Signs]]).
- **DPP-4 inhibitors** (sitagliptin, vildagliptin, teneligliptin): modest effect, low risk of low sugar; widely used in India.
- **SGLT2 inhibitors** (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin): make the kidneys pass extra glucose in urine; also **protect the heart and kidneys**, even in people without diabetes. Increase the risk of genital fungal infections, so hygiene matters, and need pausing during severe illness or dehydration as advised.
- **GLP-1 receptor agonists** (such as semaglutide, liraglutide; now available as injections and some as tablets): mimic the gut fullness hormone ([[04 - Hormones - The Body's Messaging System]]); lower sugar, cause significant weight loss and reduce heart events in high-risk people. Side effects include nausea. Because weight lost includes some muscle, **protein and strength training matter even more** on these drugs ([[12 - Strength Training - Muscle as Medicine]]).
- **Insulin:** the most powerful sugar-lowering treatment. *"Insulin means the end stage"* and *"insulin is addictive"* are myths. It's used when the pancreas can't keep up, sometimes temporarily. Starting it in time prevents complications.

**Myth:** *"Diabetes medicines damage the kidneys."* High sugar damages kidneys; some medicines need dose adjustment when kidneys are already weak, which is where the myth comes from.

## 5. Aspirin and blood thinners

- **After a heart attack, stent, bypass or ischaemic stroke:** **antiplatelet** medicines (aspirin, clopidogrel, ticagrelor) are essential. Stopping them early after a stent can cause sudden stent blockage and a heart attack. Never stop without asking the cardiologist, including before dental or other procedures.
- **For people without heart disease** (primary prevention): routine daily aspirin is **no longer recommended for most people**, especially older adults, because bleeding risk outweighs benefit. It's a doctor's decision for selected high-risk people.
- **Anticoagulants** (warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran) are used for atrial fibrillation, clots in legs or lungs, and some valve problems. They prevent strokes but raise bleeding risk; warfarin needs regular **INR** blood tests and interacts with many foods and medicines.
- **Avoid NSAID painkillers** with blood thinners unless a doctor approves, as the combination raises bleeding risk.

## 6. Thyroid medicine

**Levothyroxine** replaces missing thyroid hormone. Take it on an empty stomach, 30–60 minutes before breakfast and tea, and 4 hours apart from calcium, iron and antacids. Recheck TSH 6–8 weeks after any dose change ([[22 - Thyroid, Anaemia & Common Deficiencies]]). It's usually lifelong; it doesn't "weaken" the thyroid further.

## 7. The medicines people take too casually

### Antibiotics
- Don't work against viruses (colds, most coughs, flu, dengue, most diarrhoea).
- Take only with a prescription, at the right dose and times, for the full prescribed course.
- Over-use breeds **antimicrobial resistance**, a serious and growing cause of death in India ([[17 - Infections, Hygiene & Vaccines]]).

### Painkillers
- **Paracetamol:** safe at the right dose (for most adults, generally up to 3–4 g a day in divided doses, less with liver disease, heavy alcohol use or low body weight). Overdose, including accidentally taking several cold/flu products that all contain paracetamol, can cause liver failure.
- **NSAIDs** (ibuprofen, diclofenac, aceclofenac, nimesulide, etoricoxib): useful short term, but long-term or high-dose use risks stomach ulcers and bleeding, kidney damage, raised BP and heart risk ([[08 - The Liver & Kidneys - Factories and Filters]], [[21 - Joints & Bones - Osteoarthritis, Osteoporosis & Falls]]). Gels on the skin are much safer for joint pain.

### Acid suppressants (PPIs)
Pantoprazole, omeprazole, rabeprazole and others are excellent for ulcers and reflux, but are often taken daily for years "for gas." Long-term use is linked to lower B12, magnesium, calcium and iron absorption, more gut infections and possibly bone loss. Review with a doctor whether they're still needed; stop gradually if advised, since rebound acidity is common after sudden stopping ([[07 - Gut Health & Digestion]]).

### Steroids
Steroids (prednisolone, dexamethasone, betamethasone and others) are powerful and sometimes lifesaving, but long-term or repeated courses raise blood sugar, BP, weight, infection risk, cataracts and bone loss, and suppress the body's own cortisol. **Never stop long-term steroids suddenly.** Some unregulated "instant relief" powders and tablets for joint pain, asthma or skin problems contain hidden steroids.

### Sleeping pills and sedatives
Alprazolam, clonazepam, lorazepam, zolpidem and similar medicines cause dependence and falls in older adults. They should be short-term and supervised ([[14 - Sleep & Circadian Rhythm]]).

### Cough syrups and "tonics"
Many combination cough syrups add little benefit; some contain codeine or sedating antihistamines. "Tonics" and appetite stimulants sometimes contain hidden steroids or cyproheptadine. Use only when prescribed.

## 8. Traditional, herbal and "natural" products

Many Indian families use ayurvedic, homeopathic, unani or herbal products alongside modern medicine.
- **"Natural" isn't the same as safe.** Some unregulated products have contained heavy metals (lead, mercury, arsenic) or hidden steroids and painkillers, and some herbs damage the liver or interact with medicines (for example, affecting blood thinners or blood sugar).
- **Don't stop prescribed medicines** for diabetes, BP, thyroid, heart disease or epilepsy in favour of herbal cures.
- **Tell every doctor** about everything taken, including supplements, whey protein, herbal powders and home remedies.
- **Buy from licensed sources** and check AYUSH licence numbers on traditional medicines.

## 9. Taking medicines well

- **Know each medicine**: its name (generic name, not just brand), why you take it, the dose and timing, and its main side effects.
- **A written medicine list** in the health card, updated after every change ([[32 - The Family Health System - Putting It All Together]]).
- **A weekly pill organiser** (a box with compartments for each day) prevents missed or double doses, especially for older adults taking several medicines.
- **Phone alarms** or linking doses to daily routines (with morning tea, after dinner).
- **Don't stop or change doses** without asking, even when feeling fine.
- **Ask before adding anything**, including over-the-counter painkillers, cold remedies, antacids and supplements.
- **Bring all medicines to doctor visits** once a year (a "brown bag review") to check for duplicates and interactions. Older adults on five or more medicines (**polypharmacy**) are at higher risk of side effects, falls and confusion; regular review often allows some to be stopped safely.
- **Store medicines properly**: cool, dry, away from children; check expiry dates.

## 10. Saving money without cutting corners

- **Generic medicines** contain the same active ingredient as branded ones. **Pradhan Mantri Bhartiya Janaushadhi Kendras** sell quality-tested generics at a fraction of brand prices.
- **Ask the doctor to write generic names**, and ask whether a cheaper equivalent exists.
- **Combination tablets** (for example, two BP medicines in one tablet) can reduce cost and pill burden.
- **Government schemes:** free BP and diabetes medicines through the India Hypertension Control Initiative and NP-NCD at many government health centres; Ayushman Bharat for hospital care ([[30 - Health Insurance & Money for Health]]).

---

## The layer, in one line

Medicines and lifestyle work together: statins, BP and diabetes medicines and antiplatelets after heart events save lives when taken daily and never stopped without advice, while antibiotics, painkillers, acid suppressants, steroids, sleeping pills and unregulated herbal products cause real harm when taken casually — so know every medicine, keep a list, review it yearly, and use generics to save money.
