# SLI, SLO and Error Budgets

#backend #observability #reliability

> **In one line:** Pick a small number of metrics that reflect what users actually experience, set targets you are willing to be held to, and treat the gap between the target and 100% as a budget you are allowed to spend.

---

## The Idea

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **SLI** | Service Level *Indicator* — a measurement of user experience |
| **SLO** | Service Level *Objective* — the target for that measurement |
| **SLA** | Service Level *Agreement* — a contract, with consequences |
| **Error budget** | `100% − SLO`. How much you are permitted to fail |

```
SLI:  proportion of document reads completing in under 500ms
SLO:  99.5% over a rolling 28 days
Error budget: 0.5% → about 3.4 hours of failure per 28 days
```

💡 **The insight that makes SLOs useful: 100% is the wrong target.** It is unachievable, and pursuing it means never shipping anything. **An error budget converts reliability from an argument into arithmetic** — you have 3.4 hours, and you decide how to spend them.

⚠️ **An SLO nobody acts on is a dashboard, not an SLO.** The consequence — usually "stop feature work and fix reliability when the budget is exhausted" — **is what makes it real.**

---

## Choosing SLIs

**An SLI must measure what a user experiences**, not what your infrastructure is doing.

```
❌  CPU utilisation           — a user does not care
❌  Cache hit ratio           — internal
❌  Queue depth               — internal
✅  Request success rate      — the user sees failures
✅  Request latency           — the user sees slowness
✅  Time from upload to searchable   — the user waits for this
```

### The standard forms

| Type | SLI | For |
|---|---|---|
| **Availability** | good requests / total requests | Request-driven services |
| **Latency** | requests faster than threshold / total | Request-driven services |
| **Freshness** | data younger than threshold / total | Pipelines, derived stores |
| **Correctness** | correct records / total | Data processing |
| **Coverage** | records processed / records expected | Batch jobs |

💡 **Latency as a *proportion* rather than a percentile is the formulation that works.** "99% of requests under 500ms" composes with an error budget; "p99 under 500ms" does not, because you cannot add up percentile violations over a month.

**Both express the same thing. Only one is budgetable.**

### The threshold is a product decision

⚠️ **Do not set the threshold from your current p99.** That enshrines today's performance as the target regardless of whether users find it acceptable.

```
"How slow does a document page have to be before a user notices
 and is annoyed?"
```

💡 **Ask the product team, or look at behavioural data** — the latency at which users abandon. **Then check whether you currently meet it.** If not, that is information, not a reason to move the threshold.

### Keep the number small

⚠️ **Three to five SLOs per service.** More than that and nobody knows which matter, error budget policy becomes incoherent, and the whole thing becomes paperwork.

**Cover the critical user journeys**, not every endpoint.

## The Error Budget

```
SLO: 99.5% over 28 days
Budget: 0.5% × 28 days = 3.36 hours
Consumed so far: 47 minutes (23%)
Remaining: 2.58 hours
```

### The policy is the point

```markdown
## Error budget policy

**Budget healthy (> 50% remaining)**
Ship normally. Take reasonable risks.

**Budget low (10–50% remaining)**
Increase deploy caution. Prioritise reliability work in planning.

**Budget exhausted (< 10%)**
Feature freeze. Only reliability work and critical fixes ship
until the budget recovers.

**Budget exhausted twice in a quarter**
Re-examine the SLO target or the architecture.
```

💡 **The last clause matters.** Repeatedly exhausting the budget means either the target is unrealistic for your architecture, or the architecture needs to change. **Both are useful conclusions, and neither is "try harder".**

⚠️ **A policy nobody has agreed to in advance will not be followed during a freeze.** Get sign-off from whoever can stop feature work, **before** you need it.

### Spending the budget deliberately

💡 **The reframe that changes behaviour: leftover budget is wasted capacity for change.**

| Deliberate spend | |
|---|---|
| A risky migration | You have budget for it |
| **Chaos experiments in production** | Consuming budget on purpose ([[11.9 - Chaos and Failure Injection]]) |
| A faster deploy cadence | More change, more risk |
| Removing a redundant safety mechanism | |

⚠️ **A team consistently at 90% budget remaining is being too conservative**, and that has a cost in shipping speed nobody measures.

### Burn rate

```
burn_rate = (error rate) / (1 - SLO)
```

```
SLO 99.5% → budget 0.5%
Current error rate 5% → burn rate 10× → the 28-day budget is gone in 2.8 days
```

💡 **Burn rate is what you alert on, not the raw error rate.** It normalises across SLOs, so one alerting rule works for a 99.9% target and a 99% one.

### Multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerting

```yaml
- alert: ErrorBudgetBurnFast
  expr: burn_rate_1h > 14.4 and burn_rate_5m > 14.4
  severity: page          # consumes 2% of a 30-day budget in 1 hour

- alert: ErrorBudgetBurnSlow
  expr: burn_rate_6h > 6 and burn_rate_30m > 6
  severity: page

- alert: ErrorBudgetBurnGradual
  expr: burn_rate_24h > 3 and burn_rate_2h > 3
  severity: ticket
```

⚠️ **The two-window requirement is what stops flapping.** A one-hour burn rate stays elevated long after the incident ends; **requiring the short window to also be elevated means the alert resolves when the problem does.**

💡 **This is the alerting design from the SRE Workbook and it is the best available.** It catches both a sudden total outage and a slow leak, with different urgency, from one set of rules.

## Measuring the SLI

### Where you measure changes the answer

```
Load balancer     ← closest to the user, misses what never arrived
Application       ← misses network and LB failures
Client-side (RUM) ← the truth, and hardest to collect
```

💡 **Measure at the load balancer as the default.** Application-side metrics cannot see requests that failed to reach you, **which is exactly what happens in the worst incidents.**

⚠️ **A service reporting 99.99% availability from its own metrics while users see errors is the classic gray failure** ([[11.1 - How Systems Fail]]). **Add synthetic checks as a cross-check.**

### What counts as a failure

```python
# Deliberate decisions
5xx                          → failure
429 (rate limited)           → NOT a failure. Working as designed
4xx (client error)           → NOT a failure
499 (client disconnect)      → NOT a failure
Degraded response            → ⚠️ decide explicitly
```

⚠️ **The degraded case needs a decision.** A document page loading without related documents because the search service is down — **is that a success?**

💡 **Usually yes, and you should track it separately.** Otherwise a degradation that users barely notice burns the same budget as a total outage, and your budget stops reflecting user pain.

**Write the definition down.** "Good" is a judgement, and it must be made once rather than re-litigated during an incident.

## In Production

### The recording rules

```yaml
- record: sli:document_read_good:rate5m
  expr: sum(rate(http_requests_total{route="/documents/:id",status!~"5.."}[5m]))

- record: sli:document_read_total:rate5m
  expr: sum(rate(http_requests_total{route="/documents/:id"}[5m]))

- record: slo:document_read:ratio_rate5m
  expr: sli:document_read_good:rate5m / sli:document_read_total:rate5m
```

💡 **Define SLIs as recording rules, not ad-hoc dashboard queries.** They become the single definition used by dashboards, alerts and reports — **and when someone asks "what counts as good", there is one answer.**

### The error budget dashboard

```
Document read availability      SLO 99.5%
  28-day compliance             99.71%     ✅
  Budget remaining              42%
  Burn rate (1h)                0.8×
  Projected exhaustion          —

Document read latency (<500ms)  SLO 99.0%
  28-day compliance             98.82%     ⚠️
  Budget remaining              -18%       BREACHED
  Burn rate (1h)                2.1×
  Projected exhaustion          exhausted
```

💡 **"Projected exhaustion" is the number that drives behaviour.** "You will exhaust your budget in 4 days at the current rate" prompts action; "99.2% compliance" does not.

### Composing SLOs across dependencies

```
4 services at 99.9% each, chained synchronously → 99.6%
```

⚠️ **You cannot set an SLO your architecture cannot meet** ([[10.3 - Communication Patterns]]).

```python
achievable = product(availability[dep] for dep in required_sync_dependencies)
```

💡 **Compute this before committing to a target.** If the arithmetic says 99.6% and you promised 99.9%, **the fix is architectural — make dependencies optional or asynchronous — not a monitoring change.**

**This calculation belongs in the SLO document itself.**

### SLO versus SLA

⚠️ **Never set your internal SLO equal to your external SLA.** Breaching the SLO would mean breaching the contract simultaneously, with no warning period.

```
SLA (contract):  99.5%, with service credits
SLO (internal):  99.9%
```

💡 **The gap is your warning zone.** Burning the internal budget alerts you long before the contractual one is at risk.

### Rolling out SLOs

```
1. Pick 3 SLIs for the critical journeys
2. Measure for 4 weeks. Set NO targets
3. Look at the distribution. Set targets slightly above current performance
4. Run for 4 more weeks with alerting but no policy
5. Agree the error budget policy with stakeholders
6. Enforce it
```

⚠️ **Setting a target before measuring produces either a target you always meet — useless — or one you never meet, which gets ignored within a month.**

💡 **Step 4 is where most of the learning happens.** You discover which alerts are noisy, which SLIs do not reflect user pain, and which thresholds are wrong, **before anyone's roadmap depends on them.**

### Conduit's SLOs

| Journey | SLI | SLO |
|---|---|---|
| **Document read** | Requests returning 2xx/3xx/4xx in < 500ms | 99.5% |
| **Search** | Searches returning results in < 1s | 99.0% |
| **Upload to searchable** | Documents indexed within 5 minutes | 95.0% |
| Ingestion correctness | Documents indexed without error | 99.5% |

💡 **Four SLOs, four different types** — availability, latency, freshness and correctness. **The freshness one is the interesting one**, because it spans the whole asynchronous pipeline and is the thing users actually complain about ([[08.1 - Why Background Work Exists]]).

⚠️ **Search is deliberately 99.0%, not 99.5%, because search is an optional dependency that degrades** ([[11.6 - Graceful Degradation]]). **Setting the same target for an optional and a required path means the optional one dominates your budget.**

**The 95% on freshness looks low and is deliberate** — 5% of documents taking longer than 5 minutes is acceptable for large files, and setting 99% would mean paging someone every time a 200MB PDF arrives.

**Part 1 — Define four SLIs.** One of each type — availability, latency, freshness, correctness — for Conduit's critical journeys.

**Write the "good" definition precisely for each**, including the decisions: are 429s failures, are 4xx failures, are degraded responses good?

**Then implement them as Prometheus recording rules**, measured at the load balancer where possible.

**Part 2 — Baseline for four weeks.** Or simulate four weeks of realistic traffic including a few incidents.

**Set no targets.** Chart the distribution of each SLI over the period.

**Then set targets slightly above current performance and compute what the error budget would have been.** How many times would you have breached?

**Part 3 — Where you measure changes the answer.** Compute the same availability SLI from application metrics, load balancer metrics, and synthetic checks.

**Inject a failure between the load balancer and the application. Confirm the application-side SLI does not see it.**

**Report the difference. That gap is why measurement point matters.**

**Part 4 — The burn rate calculator.** Implement burn rate over multiple windows and the budget dashboard, including projected exhaustion.

**Then simulate three incident shapes** — a 10-minute total outage, a 6-hour 5% error rate, and a 3-day 1% error rate — and confirm the dashboard reflects each correctly.

**Part 5 — Tune the alerting.** Implement the three-tier multi-window alerting.

Replay your four weeks of data. **Count: true positives, false positives, and missed incidents.**

**Then remove the second window from each rule and replay again. Count the flapping.**

**Chart alert count with and without the two-window requirement.**

**Part 6 — Achievable versus promised.** Compute your architecture's achievable availability from the required synchronous dependency chain.

**Compare it to your SLO target.**

If the arithmetic says you cannot meet it, **change the architecture** — make a dependency optional or asynchronous — and recompute. **Report the improvement.**

**Part 7 — The policy.** Write the error budget policy with all four tiers.

**Then simulate a quarter:** run incidents that exhaust the budget in month two. **Follow the policy** — freeze feature work, do reliability work, and measure whether the budget recovers.

**Write down what the freeze would have cost in shipped features**, and whether the trade was right.

**Part 8 — Defend it.** Write a one-page document for each SLO: what it measures, why that threshold, what the budget policy is, and what the architecture can actually achieve.

**Then have someone challenge each threshold and see whether your justification holds.**

**Done when:**
- Four SLIs of four types are defined as recording rules with precise "good" definitions
- You have four weeks of baseline data and targets derived from it
- The measurement-point comparison shows a gap you can quantify
- Your dashboard shows projected exhaustion correctly for three incident shapes
- Two-window alerting demonstrably eliminates flapping your data would produce
- Achievable availability is computed, and the architecture changed if it fell short
- A simulated budget exhaustion was followed by an actual freeze and recovery
- Every SLO threshold survives being challenged
