# Blood Sugar, Insulin Resistance & Diabetes

*Health & Fitness · 2026-10-30*

India is often called the diabetes capital of the world, and it isn't because Indians eat more sweets than everyone else. It's because of a quiet, decades-long process called insulin resistance, which starts years before any blood test turns red and sits underneath diabetes, most heart attacks, fatty liver and a lot more. Understand this one mechanism and half of preventive health makes sense.

---

## 1. Blood sugar is a tightly controlled number

[[01 - Human Body & Nutrition Basics]] covered how carbohydrates break down into glucose, and [[03 - Energy Metabolism - How the Body Makes and Uses Fuel]] showed how cells burn and store it. What matters now is that the body treats the amount of glucose in your blood as something to be kept within a narrow band, the way a thermostat holds a room's temperature.

An adult has only about a teaspoon (4–5 g) of glucose dissolved in their entire bloodstream at any moment. A single plate of rice and rotis can deliver 60–100 g of carbohydrate. So after every meal the body has a logistics problem: move a flood of glucose out of the blood and into cells, fast, before it does damage.

Too little blood glucose (**hypoglycaemia**) starves the brain, which is why it causes shaking, confusion and fainting within minutes. Too much (**hyperglycaemia**), sustained over years, slowly damages blood vessels, nerves, kidneys and eyes. The body fights hard to avoid both.

## 2. Insulin is the key that opens the cell door

[[04 - Hormones - The Body's Messaging System]] introduced insulin as a hormone. Here's its role in detail. **Insulin** is made by beta cells in the pancreas (a gland tucked behind the stomach). When blood glucose rises after a meal, insulin is released and does three main things:

- **Opens the doors.** It signals muscle and fat cells to pull glucose in. Muscle is the biggest destination: under insulin's influence, skeletal muscle absorbs the large majority of the glucose from a meal. This one fact is why [[12 - Strength Training - Muscle as Medicine]] matters so much for diabetes.
- **Tells the liver to stop.** Between meals the liver releases stored glucose to keep levels steady. Insulin tells it to pause and store instead.
- **Says "store, don't burn."** Insulin shifts the body into storage mode. Spare glucose becomes **glycogen** (a compact glucose store in liver and muscle) and, once those stores are full, fat.

Its opposite is **glucagon**, which tells the liver to release glucose when levels drop. Together they're the thermostat.

## 3. Insulin resistance: when the cells stop listening

**Insulin resistance** is the state where cells respond weakly to insulin. The key turns, but the door only half-opens. The pancreas responds the only way it can: by making more insulin. For years, often a decade or more, this works. Blood sugar looks normal on a test because the pancreas is shouting to keep it there.

This is the most important thing to understand about diabetes: **the disease starts long before the sugar number rises.** A normal fasting sugar can hide a pancreas working at double or triple its normal output. The blood test only catches the problem when the pancreas starts losing the fight.

What drives cells to stop listening:

- **Fat in the wrong places.** Fat stored under the skin is relatively harmless. Fat packed around the organs (**visceral fat**) and inside the liver and muscle cells interferes directly with insulin signalling. Visceral fat is metabolically active: it releases inflammatory signals ([[06 - The Immune System & Inflammation]]) and fatty acids that blunt insulin's effect.
- **Too little muscle, and muscle that isn't used.** Less muscle means less storage space for glucose. Inactive muscle also responds less well to insulin; a single bout of exercise improves insulin sensitivity for a day or two.
- **A constant flood of fast carbohydrate.** Repeated large surges of glucose and insulin, meal after meal, year after year.
- **Poor sleep and chronic stress.** The stress hormone **cortisol** raises blood sugar and opposes insulin. Even a few nights of short sleep measurably worsen insulin sensitivity ([[14 - Sleep & Circadian Rhythm]], [[15 - Stress, Mental Health & Social Connection]]).
- **Tobacco.** Nicotine worsens insulin resistance, and tobacco users have a higher risk of type 2 diabetes ([[16 - Tobacco, Alcohol & Environmental Exposures]]).
- **Genes and early life.** Some populations, including South Asians, are more prone to it. More on that below.

## 4. The "thin-fat Indian"

South Asians develop insulin resistance and diabetes at lower body weights and younger ages than Europeans. The Indian diabetologist Chittaranjan Yajnik described this as the **thin-fat** phenotype (a phenotype is an observable trait): at the same BMI, the average Indian carries more body fat, more of it visceral, and less muscle.

Two consequences matter for families:

- **BMI misleads.** **BMI** (body-mass index, weight in kg divided by height in metres squared) was built on European populations. An Indian with a "normal" BMI can still be metabolically unhealthy. For Asian Indians, a BMI of 23 or more is already treated as overweight in Indian guidelines.
- **Waist size is the better cheap test.** A measuring tape around the belly at the navel is a rough proxy for visceral fat. For South Asians, a waist of **90 cm (about 35 inches) or more for men** and **80 cm (about 31.5 inches) or more for women** is the commonly used warning line. This costs nothing and is worth measuring every month.

Early life matters too. Babies born small because their mothers were undernourished in pregnancy are more prone to insulin resistance later, especially if they then gain weight quickly in childhood. This is one reason [[29 - Health Across Life Stages]] puts so much weight on pregnancy and early childhood nutrition.

## 5. The progression: from normal to diabetes

Type 2 diabetes is a slope, not a switch:

1. **Insulin resistance with normal sugar.** Invisible on routine tests. Clues: a growing waist, high triglycerides with low HDL, fatty liver on an ultrasound, dark velvety skin patches on the neck or armpits (**acanthosis nigricans**).
2. **Prediabetes.** The pancreas is starting to lose. Sugar is above normal but below the diabetes line. This stage is often reversible with lifestyle change.
3. **Type 2 diabetes.** Sugar stays high. The beta cells are now also wearing out, not just facing resistance.
4. **Complications.** After years of high sugar: damage to small vessels (eyes, kidneys, nerves) and large vessels (heart attack, stroke).

**Type 1 diabetes** is a different disease: the immune system destroys the beta cells, usually in childhood or young adulthood, and insulin injections are essential from day one. Everything in this note is about type 2, which makes up the vast majority of adult cases.

## 6. The numbers on a report

Three common tests, with the standard cutoffs (American Diabetes Association, followed by Indian labs):

| Test | What it measures | Normal | Prediabetes | Diabetes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fasting plasma glucose** | Sugar after 8–12 hours without food | below 100 mg/dL | 100–125 | 126 or more |
| **HbA1c** | Average sugar over ~3 months | below 5.7% | 5.7–6.4% | 6.5% or more |
| **2-hour glucose after 75 g glucose drink (OGTT)** | How well the body clears a sugar load | below 140 mg/dL | 140–199 | 200 or more |

**HbA1c** deserves an explanation. Glucose in the blood sticks to haemoglobin (the oxygen-carrying protein inside red blood cells, [[05 - Blood, Circulation & the Heart]]). Red cells live about three months, so the percentage of haemoglobin with sugar stuck to it reflects your average sugar over that period. It's harder to fool than a fasting test, because skipping dinner the night before doesn't change it. Two caveats: anaemia and some haemoglobin variants can make HbA1c misleading, which is one reason doctors look at both HbA1c and glucose.

A diagnosis normally needs two abnormal results, either the same test repeated or two different tests agreeing.

**Fasting insulin** and **HOMA-IR** (a score calculated from fasting glucose and fasting insulin) can reveal insulin resistance at stage 1, before sugar rises. They aren't standard screening tests and labs vary, but they can be useful for people with a strong family history and "normal" sugar.

## 7. Why high sugar is so destructive

Glucose is sticky. Over years, excess glucose attaches to proteins throughout the body, forming **AGEs** (advanced glycation end-products, essentially sugar-damaged proteins). It damages the thin inner lining of blood vessels and makes them stiff and inflamed. The result:

- **Eyes:** diabetic retinopathy (damage to the light-sensing layer at the back of the eye), a leading cause of preventable blindness. It's silent until late, so a yearly eye check for people with diabetes is standard.
- **Kidneys:** diabetic kidney disease ([[20 - Blood Pressure, Stroke & Kidney Disease]]). The earliest sign is a tiny amount of protein leaking into urine, measured by **UACR** (urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio). A routine urine strip test misses this early stage.
- **Nerves:** numbness and burning in the feet. Combined with poor circulation, a small unnoticed cut can become a non-healing ulcer. Daily foot checks are a real, lifesaving habit for people with diabetes.
- **Heart and brain:** diabetes roughly doubles the risk of heart attack and stroke ([[19 - How Heart Disease Actually Happens]]).

There's also a less visible effect: insulin resistance itself, even before diabetes, pushes the liver to make more triglycerides, lowers HDL and creates small, dense LDL particles. That's the classic Indian lipid report: high triglyceride/HDL ratio, low HDL, LDL that "looks okay." The sugar problem and the cholesterol problem are often the same problem.

## 8. What actually reverses the slope

The good news is that insulin resistance responds strongly to behaviour. In the large **Diabetes Prevention Program** trial in the US, lifestyle change (modest weight loss and about 150 minutes of walking a week) cut the progression from prediabetes to diabetes by over half. An Indian trial (the Indian Diabetes Prevention Programme) found similar benefits.

The levers, in rough order of power:

1. **Build and use muscle.** Every kilo of muscle is extra space to park glucose, and every working session improves insulin sensitivity. See [[12 - Strength Training - Muscle as Medicine]].
2. **Lose visceral fat.** Even 5–7% of body weight, if it comes off the waist, makes a large difference.
3. **Change the plate, not just the oil.** Fewer and less refined carbohydrates per meal, more protein and fibre. See [[11 - The Indian Plate - Rebuilding the Thali]].
4. **Walk after meals.** Muscles contracting soak up glucose without needing as much insulin. Even 10–15 minutes of walking after a heavy meal visibly flattens the sugar spike.
5. **Sleep and stress.** Covered in notes 14 and 15.
6. **Stop tobacco.** Covered in note 16.
7. **Medicines when needed.** Lifestyle is the foundation, not a substitute for treatment. **Metformin**, the usual first diabetes medicine, has been used for over 60 years, is cheap, and mainly works by reducing the liver's glucose output. Newer drug classes also protect the heart and kidneys. Starting medication isn't failure; delaying it while sugar damages vessels is the real risk. See [[28 - Medicines Without Myths]].

A common Indian myth worth killing: *"Diabetes medicines damage the kidneys."* Uncontrolled diabetes damages the kidneys. Most of these medicines are adjusted when kidney function is already low, which is where the confusion comes from.

## 9. Three things families get wrong

- **"No sweets means no diabetes risk."** Rice, roti, poha, bread and potatoes all become glucose. Two extra rotis can matter more than a spoon of sugar in tea.
- **"I'm thin, so I'm safe."** The thin-fat phenotype means many lean Indians are insulin resistant. Measure waist and test sugar regardless of weight.
- **"Sugar is fine, so I don't need to test again."** Insulin resistance progresses silently. Anyone over 30 in India, or younger with a family history, should check HbA1c and fasting sugar yearly ([[27 - The Testing Calendar]]).

---

## The layer, in one line

Diabetes isn't a sugar problem that appears one day; it's the late visible stage of insulin resistance, driven mainly by visceral fat, unused muscle, large refined-carb meals, poor sleep and tobacco, and it's most reversible in the years before the number turns red.
