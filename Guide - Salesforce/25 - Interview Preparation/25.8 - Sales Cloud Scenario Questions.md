# Sales Cloud Scenario Questions

## What these rounds test

Not whether you know what an Opportunity is. Whether you can take a business requirement, ask the right questions, and choose between mechanisms with reasons.

**The pattern that works:** clarify, then design, then name the tradeoff you accepted.

---

## Scenario 1

> Sales reps complain that lead follow-up is inconsistent. Some leads sit untouched for a week.

**Questions first.** How many leads a day? Where do they come from — web, events, a partner? Is there an existing assignment mechanism? What is the expected response time, and is it different by source or region?

**The design.**

Lead assignment rules for routing, ordered by criteria — web leads to a queue by region, event leads to whoever ran the event. Assignment rules run on insert automatically from the UI, and **not from the API unless the assignment header is set** — which is the detail that catches integrations creating leads that silently stay with the integration user.

A queue rather than direct assignment where ownership should be pulled rather than pushed, so a rep who is unavailable does not block a lead.

Then the follow-up gap. A time-based mechanism — a scheduled path on a record-triggered flow, firing 24 hours after creation, checking whether the status has moved and escalating if not. Scheduled paths are per-record, which is the right shape here; a nightly sweep over all leads is a worse fit.

**The tradeoff I would name:** escalation notifications become noise if the threshold is wrong. I would want the threshold in Custom Metadata so it can be tuned without a deployment, and I would measure the actual response distribution before setting it rather than guessing.

## Scenario 2

> Management wants to know why deals are lost, and reps rarely fill in the reason.

**The instinct is a required field. That is usually the wrong answer**, because a required field on a closed-lost opportunity produces garbage — reps pick the first value to get past the screen.

**Questions.** Is the data used for anything, or is it reported on and ignored? What decision would change if the data were better?

**The design.**

A validation rule requiring the reason only when the stage becomes Closed Lost, so it does not obstruct anything else. A picklist with values the business actually distinguishes — five meaningful values, not twenty overlapping ones.

Path with guidance for success on the Closed Lost stage, explaining what each reason means, because ambiguous values produce inconsistent data regardless of enforcement.

And a dependent text field for detail on the values where the category alone is not actionable — "Lost to competitor" is not useful without knowing which.

**The tradeoff:** enforcement produces compliance, not accuracy. I would want the report reviewed for whether the distribution looks plausible, and I would expect to revise the picklist once real data arrives.

## Scenario 3

> Deals over ₹50 lakh need regional director approval. Over ₹2 crore, the CEO. Reps are bypassing it.

**Questions.** What does "bypassing" mean — submitting without approval, or editing after approval? Who can currently edit a closed opportunity? How many approvals a month?

**The design.**

An approval process with two entry criteria and a two-step chain for the higher tier, dynamic approver by role rather than a named user, so it survives people changing jobs.

The bypass is the interesting part. An approval process locks the record while pending, and **the gap is usually that a rep closes the deal without submitting at all**. So a validation rule preventing the stage moving to Closed Won above the threshold unless an approval is recorded — checking a field the approval process stamps, not the approval object directly.

Field-level security so the approved amount is not editable by the rep after approval.

**The tradeoff:** approval processes are rigid. Recalling and resubmitting is clunky, and a deal that changes value mid-approval means starting over. Where the org needs more flexibility, Flow-driven approval with a custom object is more work and more adaptable — I would choose based on how often the criteria change.

## Scenario 4

> The sales team wants forecasting, and every rep tracks their pipeline in a personal spreadsheet.

**The spreadsheet is the signal.** They do it because the system does not give them what they need, and building forecasting on top without addressing that produces a forecast nobody trusts.

**Questions.** What is in the spreadsheets that is not in Salesforce? What does the forecast get used for — quota, capacity, revenue recognition? How often does it change?

**The design.**

Collaborative Forecasting, which is the standard mechanism — forecast categories mapped to stages, quotas loaded per user per period, and the hierarchy giving managers a rolled-up view with the ability to adjust.

The adjustment capability is what usually wins reps over, because the objection is "the number is wrong and I can't fix it".

Opportunity splits if revenue is shared across reps, which is common in a team-selling model and is invisible until someone asks why the numbers do not add up.

**The thing I would add:** a forecast snapshot object, populated by a scheduled job, so historical forecast accuracy can be measured. Without it, "we forecast ₹8 crore last quarter and closed ₹6" has no evidence, and forecast quality never improves.

**The tradeoff:** forecast categories add a concept reps have to understand. In a simple pipeline, stage-based reporting may be enough, and I would not introduce forecasting where a report would do.

## Scenario 5

> A partner channel is being launched. Partners need to register deals and see their own opportunities but not each other's.

**Questions.** How many partners, and how many users each? Do partners need to see their colleagues' deals, or only their own? Is there deal registration with protection periods?

**The design.**

The licence type decides the sharing model, and it is decided first. **Partner Community gives roles and sharing rules; Customer Community does not** — and discovering mid-project that a partner admin needs to see their team's deals, on a licence with no role hierarchy, is expensive.

Each partner account gets an account role hierarchy, which is how a partner manager sees their own users' records. That multiplies role count, which is worth noting in an org with thousands of partner accounts.

Sharing set or sharing rules scoped to the partner's account, so a partner sees only their own.

Deal registration as a custom object or an Opportunity record type, with a status and an expiry — and a validation rule preventing two partners registering the same end customer within the protection window.

**The security point I would raise:** every `@AuraEnabled` method a partner user can reach is an entry point. A component that filters by account in JavaScript and passes an unfiltered query to Apex is an enumeration vulnerability. The Apex must verify entitlement.

## Scenario 6

> Quotes are being produced in Word documents and emailed. Management wants them in Salesforce.

**Questions.** What is on a quote that is not in the opportunity? Who approves discounting? Does the quote need to be a formal document, and does it need a signature?

**The design.**

Standard Quotes, with quote line items syncing to the opportunity so the amount stays consistent. The sync direction is worth being explicit about — one quote syncs at a time, and it drives the opportunity's products.

Price books for the catalogue, with discount fields on the line item and a validation rule or approval process above a threshold.

Quote templates for the document, which handles the formatting requirement without a custom solution.

**Where I would push back:** if the requirement includes complex product configuration, bundles, dependent options, or tiered pricing, standard Quotes will not do it and CPQ is the answer. That is a much larger implementation, and identifying it early rather than building three months of custom logic that recreates CPQ badly is the valuable contribution.

The test I would apply: can a rep pick the wrong combination of products? If yes, that is configuration logic, and it belongs in CPQ.

## Scenario 7

> Account managers cannot see the full customer relationship — the same customer appears as five accounts.

**Questions.** Why five? Different legal entities, different regions, or duplicates? Is the hierarchy real, or is this a data quality problem?

**Two different problems with different answers.**

**If they are duplicates:** duplicate rules and matching rules first, to stop new ones. Then quantify with an aggregate query, then merge — accepting that the master's field values win, so preserving a better value from a duplicate means updating the master before merging. And external systems hold the dead Ids, which is what an external ID field solves.

**If they are genuinely separate entities:** account hierarchy, with roll-up reporting at the parent. A custom roll-up rather than a standard one, because standard roll-up summaries do not work across a lookup self-relationship — that limitation surprises people.

**The thing to watch:** a hierarchy with one parent account holding hundreds of thousands of children is account data skew, and every operation on a child locks the parent. If the hierarchy is deep and wide, that is a design consideration rather than a modelling detail.

## Scenario 8

> Reps say Salesforce is slow and they are spending too long on data entry.

**This is the most common real complaint**, and the answer is rarely technical.

**Questions.** Which page, which action, and how long? Is it slow for everyone or one region? What are they entering that could be derived or defaulted?

**The diagnosis.** Client or server first — the Page Optimizer extension for a Lightning page, which reports slow components and server call counts.

If it is client-side: component count on the record page, uncacheable Apex, and components loading data nobody looks at. Frequently a page with fourteen components where four would do.

If it is server-side: a debug log and the limit block, usually accumulated automation on save or a non-selective query.

**The data entry half is a design problem.** Dynamic Forms to show fields conditionally rather than all at once. Default values and derived fields so reps are not typing what the system knows. Path with guidance so the required fields per stage are visible rather than discovered by a validation rule. Quick actions for common operations.

**The point I would make:** a rep entering fifteen fields where the system could derive eight is a design failure, not a training failure. And measuring — how long the page actually takes, how many clicks a common task requires — beats guessing at which of these is the problem.

---

## How to answer these well

**Clarify before designing.** Three good questions is a stronger opening than any answer.

**Reach for declarative first.** Assignment rules, approval processes, validation rules, Path, Dynamic Forms. Proposing Apex where configuration would do is a negative signal in most organizations.

**Name the volume assumption.** "This works at a hundred a day; at a hundred thousand I'd design it differently" shows you know the answer is conditional.

**Say what you traded away.** Every design gives something up, and naming it is the difference between a defensible answer and a confident one.

**Question the requirement where it deserves it.** "Reps aren't filling in the reason" may not be solved by making the field required, and saying so is more valuable than implementing it.
