# Joints & Bones: Osteoarthritis, Osteoporosis & Falls

*Health & Fitness · 2026-10-30*

"Ghutno ka dard" — knee pain — is so common among Indian mothers and grandmothers that it's treated as an inevitable part of ageing, managed with painkillers, oils and sitting still. It's actually one of the most treatable problems in later life, and sitting still is the worst thing for it. The silent partner, thinning bones, is often discovered only when a minor fall breaks a hip.

---

## 1. Osteoarthritis: what it really is

[[09 - Muscles, Bones & Joints - How the Frame Works]] explained that joint cartilage is living tissue fed by movement. **Osteoarthritis** (OA) is a disease of the **whole joint**, not just "wear and tear": the cartilage thins and roughens, the bone underneath thickens and grows small spurs (**osteophytes**), the joint lining becomes inflamed, and the surrounding muscles weaken. It most often affects the **knees**, hips, hands and spine.

### Who gets it
- **Women**, especially after menopause — knee OA is notably more common in women.
- **Age** over about 45–50.
- **Excess weight**, which loads the knees and adds inflammatory signals from fat ([[06 - The Immune System & Inflammation]]).
- **Weak thigh muscles.**
- **Previous knee injuries** (ligament or meniscus tears).
- **Alignment** problems: knock-knees, bow legs, flat feet.
- **Occupation and posture:** years of heavy physical labour, frequent deep squatting, kneeling and sitting cross-legged on the floor.
- **Family history.**

### Symptoms
- Pain with activity — walking, stairs (especially going down), getting up from the floor or a chair — that eases with rest.
- **Stiffness** in the morning or after sitting, usually lasting **less than 30 minutes**.
- Creaking or grinding (**crepitus**), mild swelling, reduced bending.
- Later: pain at rest or at night, visible deformity (bow-legged knees), limping.

### Diagnosis
Usually based on symptoms and examination in people over 45. An **X-ray** can show narrowing of the joint space and bone spurs, but X-ray appearance and pain often don't match — some people with "bad" X-rays have little pain, and vice versa. **MRI is rarely needed.** Blood tests are used mainly to rule out other causes (inflammatory arthritis, gout).

**Different conditions to rule out:**
- **Rheumatoid arthritis:** several joints (often hands and wrists, on both sides), swelling, morning stiffness lasting more than 30 minutes, tiredness. Needs a rheumatologist; early treatment prevents joint destruction.
- **Gout:** sudden, extremely painful, red, swollen joint, often the big toe; linked to high uric acid.
- **A hot, red, swollen joint with fever** may be infection and is urgent.

## 2. Treating knee osteoarthritis: the ladder

International guidelines (OARSI, ACR, NICE) agree on a ladder, starting from the bottom:

### Step 1: The core treatments for everyone
1. **Exercise** — the most important treatment. Strengthening the **quadriceps** and hip muscles, plus regular walking or cycling, reduces pain and improves function, often as much as painkillers ([[12 - Strength Training - Muscle as Medicine]]). Water-based exercise is gentle for very painful knees.
2. **Weight loss** if overweight. Losing 5–10% of body weight meaningfully reduces knee pain and improves function.
3. **Education:** understanding that movement is safe and helpful, and that pain during exercise doesn't mean damage. Mild pain (up to about 3–4 out of 10) that settles within 24 hours is acceptable.

### Step 2: Supporting measures
- **Physiotherapy:** a few sessions to design a personal programme, correct technique and progress it.
- **Footwear:** cushioned, supportive shoes; avoid worn-out chappals for long walks.
- **Walking aids:** a stick held in the hand **opposite** the painful knee reduces load on it.
- **Knee braces or sleeves** for some people.
- **Heat** for stiffness; **cold packs** for flare-ups with swelling.
- **Daily-life changes:** using a chair or a stool instead of sitting cross-legged on the floor, a western toilet or a raised commode seat instead of squatting, a stool for cooking at a higher counter, and avoiding prolonged kneeling.

### Step 3: Medicines, carefully
- **Topical NSAID gels** (such as diclofenac gel) rubbed on the knee: recommended first-line medicine, with far fewer side effects than tablets.
- **Oral NSAIDs** (ibuprofen, diclofenac, aceclofenac, etoricoxib): effective for flares, but for the **shortest time at the lowest dose**. Long-term use risks stomach ulcers and bleeding, kidney damage and higher BP and heart risk, especially in older adults and people with diabetes, BP or kidney disease ([[08 - The Liver & Kidneys - Factories and Filters]]).
- **Paracetamol** has only small benefits in OA but is sometimes used.
- **Duloxetine**, a medicine that acts on pain pathways, is an option for some people with chronic pain.
- **Steroid injections into the knee** can relieve a bad flare for weeks; they're not for frequent repeated use.

### Step 4: Surgery
**Total knee replacement** works very well for severe OA when pain and disability persist despite the steps above. Knee arthroscopy ("washing out" the knee) doesn't help typical OA and isn't recommended for it.

### Things with weak or no evidence
- **Glucosamine and chondroitin supplements:** large trials show little or no benefit over placebo for most people. Not harmful for most, but not worth a large ongoing expense.
- **Hyaluronic acid and PRP** (platelet-rich plasma) **injections:** mixed evidence; often expensive.
- **"Instant relief" ayurvedic or herbal tablets for joint pain:** some unregulated products have been found to contain **hidden steroids or heavy metals**. Dramatic quick relief from an unlabelled tablet is a warning sign, not a reassurance.
- **Massage oils** may feel soothing but don't treat the underlying problem.

## 3. Osteoporosis: the silent bone thief

**Osteoporosis** is bone that has lost so much density and structure that it breaks with minimal force — a fall from standing height, a cough, lifting a bucket ([[09 - Muscles, Bones & Joints - How the Frame Works]]). It has **no symptoms until a fracture**.

### Common fractures
- **Hip:** the most serious. A large share of older people with a hip fracture never regain their previous independence, and the risk of death in the following year is significantly raised.
- **Spine (vertebral):** often silent; shows up as loss of height, a stooped back ("dowager's hump") or sudden back pain.
- **Wrist:** often the first sign, from putting out a hand to break a fall.

### Risk factors
- **Women after menopause** (oestrogen loss speeds bone loss)
- Age over 65 (women) or 70 (men)
- Low body weight
- Previous fracture after age 50
- A parent who broke a hip
- Smoking and tobacco, heavy alcohol
- **Long-term steroid tablets** (three months or more)
- Low calcium, low vitamin D (very common in India), low protein
- Inactivity
- Early menopause
- Some conditions: rheumatoid arthritis, overactive thyroid, chronic kidney or liver disease, coeliac disease
- Some medicines: long-term acid suppressants, some anti-seizure drugs

### Testing
A **DEXA scan** (dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, a low-dose X-ray of the hip and spine) measures bone density and gives a **T-score**, which compares your bones with a healthy young adult:
- **−1.0 or higher:** normal
- **−1.0 to −2.5:** low bone mass (osteopenia)
- **−2.5 or lower:** osteoporosis

Common recommendations: DEXA for **women 65+**, **men 70+**, and younger adults (especially postmenopausal women) with risk factors. The **FRAX** tool estimates 10-year fracture risk from risk factors, with or without a DEXA result.

### Prevention and treatment
- **Strength and weight-bearing exercise** and **balance training** ([[12 - Strength Training - Muscle as Medicine]], [[13 - Cardio, Mobility & Balance]]).
- **Calcium** from food: dairy, ragi, sesame, green leafy vegetables, paneer, tofu. Supplements if diet is short, as advised by a doctor; most adults need around 1,000–1,200 mg a day in total from all sources.
- **Vitamin D:** test and correct deficiency, as prescribed. Don't self-dose with very high-dose weekly sachets indefinitely without testing.
- **Enough protein** ([[11 - The Indian Plate - Rebuilding the Thali]]).
- **No tobacco; little or no alcohol.**
- **Medicines** when risk is high: **bisphosphonates** (such as alendronate, taken weekly, or zoledronic acid, given as a yearly drip) are effective, inexpensive and reduce fractures substantially. Other options exist for severe cases. They need a doctor's assessment and good dental health.

## 4. Falls: where joints and bones meet

Falls are where weak muscles, poor balance and thin bones combine into a life-changing event. About a third of people over 65 fall each year.

### Why older people fall
- Weak legs and poor balance
- Painful joints, limping
- Poor eyesight (cataracts, wrong glasses)
- Numb feet (diabetes, B12 deficiency, [[10 - The Brain & Nervous System]])
- **Medicines:** sleeping pills, some BP medicines (especially causing dizziness on standing), sedatives, too many medicines in total
- Dizziness from dehydration or low blood pressure on standing up quickly
- Night-time trips to the toilet in the dark
- Slippery bathroom floors, loose rugs, clutter, poor lighting, stairs without rails
- Unsuitable footwear (loose chappals)

### Preventing falls
- **Strength and balance exercises**, at least 3 times a week — the most effective single step ([[13 - Cardio, Mobility & Balance]]). Tai chi is well studied.
- **Home safety:** night lights to the toilet, non-slip bathroom mats, grab bars by the toilet and bathing area, handrails on stairs, clear floors, a sturdy stool for bathing.
- **Medicine review** with a doctor, especially sleeping pills and BP tablets.
- **Get up slowly**: sit on the bed edge for a moment before standing.
- **Eye checks** and **cataract surgery** when needed.
- **Well-fitting footwear** with backs and good grip.
- **Treat osteoporosis**, so a fall doesn't become a fracture.

### After a fall
- **Don't get up quickly.** Check for pain, especially in the hip, wrist or head.
- **Hip pain after a fall, inability to bear weight, or a leg that looks shortened or turned outwards** suggests a hip fracture — call for help; don't try to walk.
- **Any head injury** in someone on blood thinners, or followed by headache, vomiting, drowsiness or confusion, needs urgent medical review.
- **Every fall deserves a doctor's review** of why it happened, even if there's no injury: a first fall is a warning.
- Practise **how to get up from the floor** safely (roll to the side, then onto hands and knees, crawl to a sturdy chair, and push up) while still strong enough.

---

## The layer, in one line

Knee osteoarthritis is a treatable whole-joint disease whose first and best treatments are strengthening exercise, weight loss and smart daily-life changes, with medicines used carefully; osteoporosis silently thins bone until a minor fall breaks it, and the combination of strength, balance, vitamin D and calcium, home safety and medicine review prevents the falls that end independence.
