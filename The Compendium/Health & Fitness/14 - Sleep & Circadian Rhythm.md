# Sleep & Circadian Rhythm

*Health & Fitness · 2026-10-30*

Sleep isn't the absence of activity; it's when the body does its maintenance: repairing muscle, consolidating memory, resetting hormones and clearing waste from the brain. Skimp on it and blood sugar rises, appetite rises, blood pressure rises and willpower falls. Most people who say they "function fine on 5 hours" are simply used to functioning worse.

---

## 1. The body clock

Almost every cell runs on a roughly 24-hour internal clock, the **circadian rhythm** (from Latin *circa diem*, "about a day"). A master clock in the brain's hypothalamus keeps the others in sync ([[10 - The Brain & Nervous System]]).

The master clock is set mainly by **light**:
- **Morning light** (especially daylight) sets the clock and makes it easier to sleep that night.
- **Darkness** in the evening triggers **melatonin**, the hormone that signals "night" ([[04 - Hormones - The Body's Messaging System]]).
- **Bright light at night**, particularly the blue-rich light from phones, TVs and LED bulbs, suppresses melatonin and pushes the clock later.

The clock drives daily rhythms in body temperature, cortisol (high in the morning, low at night), blood pressure (normally dips at night), digestion and insulin sensitivity (better earlier in the day). That last point is one reason large late dinners raise blood sugar more than the same meal at lunch.

Other cues that help set the clock: **meal timing**, **physical activity** and **social routine**. Regular timing for all of them keeps the rhythm strong.

## 2. Two forces that make you sleepy

1. **Sleep pressure.** While you're awake, a chemical called **adenosine** builds up in the brain, increasing the urge to sleep. Sleep clears it. **Caffeine** works by blocking adenosine's effect, which is why tea or coffee late in the day delays and lightens sleep. Caffeine's effects last many hours; for sensitive people, avoiding it after early afternoon helps.
2. **The circadian signal**, which promotes wakefulness during the day and sleep at night.

Good sleep happens when high sleep pressure and the night-time circadian signal line up. Long afternoon naps or irregular schedules pull them apart.

## 3. The stages of sleep

A night of sleep cycles through stages roughly every 90 minutes:
- **Light sleep (N1, N2):** transition and the bulk of the night.
- **Deep sleep (N3, slow-wave sleep):** most of it in the first half of the night. Growth hormone release, tissue repair, immune function, and the brain's waste-clearing system are most active ([[10 - The Brain & Nervous System]]).
- **REM sleep** (rapid eye movement): mostly in the second half of the night. Vivid dreams, emotional processing and memory consolidation.

Cutting sleep short at the end of the night mainly costs REM sleep; going to bed very late after alcohol mainly disturbs deep sleep. Older adults naturally get less deep sleep and wake more often, but they still need roughly the same total sleep.

## 4. How much sleep

Recommendations from sleep-medicine bodies:
- **Adults (18–64):** 7–9 hours
- **Older adults (65+):** 7–8 hours
- **Teenagers:** 8–10 hours
- **School-age children:** 9–12 hours
- **Younger children and infants:** more

**Consistency matters as much as hours.** Going to bed and waking at roughly the same time every day, including weekends, keeps the body clock strong.

## 5. What poor sleep does

Even a few nights of short sleep cause measurable changes:
- **Blood sugar:** insulin sensitivity falls; people sleeping too little are at higher risk of type 2 diabetes ([[18 - Blood Sugar, Insulin Resistance & Diabetes]]).
- **Appetite:** ghrelin (hunger) rises and leptin (fullness) falls, driving cravings for sugary, high-calorie food.
- **Blood pressure and the heart:** short and fragmented sleep are linked with high BP, heart attacks and stroke.
- **Immunity:** weaker responses to infections and vaccines ([[06 - The Immune System & Inflammation]]).
- **Brain:** worse memory, attention, mood and decision-making; higher long-term dementia risk.
- **Muscle:** worse recovery from exercise ([[12 - Strength Training - Muscle as Medicine]]).
- **Accidents:** drowsy driving is as dangerous as drunk driving. Truck drivers, shift workers and anyone driving long distances at night are at high risk.

## 6. Common sleep problems

### Insomnia
**Insomnia** is difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep, or waking too early, with daytime effects, at least three nights a week for three months or more. It's common in older adults, women around menopause, and people with stress, anxiety, depression or chronic pain.

The most effective treatment is **CBT-I** (cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia), a structured programme that retrains sleep habits and thoughts about sleep. It works better long term than sleeping pills. Key techniques:
- Go to bed only when sleepy; get up at the same time every day.
- If not asleep within about 20 minutes, get up, do something calm in dim light, and return when sleepy.
- Use the bed only for sleep.
- Avoid long daytime naps.

**Sleeping pills** (such as alprazolam, clonazepam, zolpidem) are widely used in India, sometimes without a prescription. They can help short-term but cause dependence, next-day drowsiness, memory problems and falls in older adults, and shouldn't be used long term without medical supervision. Never stop them suddenly after long use; tapering should be guided by a doctor.

### Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA)
In **obstructive sleep apnoea**, the throat repeatedly collapses during sleep, blocking breathing for seconds at a time, dropping oxygen levels and briefly waking the brain — often hundreds of times a night without the person knowing.

Signs:
- Loud, irregular snoring with gasps, choking or pauses noticed by a bed partner
- Daytime sleepiness, falling asleep while sitting or driving
- Morning headaches, dry mouth
- High BP that's hard to control

OSA is common in India, especially with a thick neck, belly fat, a small jaw, alcohol use, and in men and postmenopausal women. It significantly raises the risk of high BP, atrial fibrillation, heart attack, stroke, diabetes and road accidents. It's diagnosed with a **sleep study** and usually treated with a **CPAP** machine (a mask that gently blows air to keep the airway open), weight loss and sleeping on the side. Loud snoring with pauses in breathing deserves a medical check, not jokes.

### Other common problems
- **Restless legs syndrome:** uncomfortable urges to move the legs in the evening; often linked to low iron.
- **Frequent night-time urination (nocturia):** common with age, an enlarged prostate in men, uncontrolled diabetes, evening fluids, and some medicines. It fragments sleep and raises fall risk in the dark.
- **Pain** from joints or back, which is worth treating actively.
- **Shift work:** irregular hours disrupt the body clock and raise metabolic and heart risks.

## 7. Building good sleep: the habits

**Daytime**
- **Get morning daylight** — 15–30 minutes outside, ideally within an hour or two of waking. For farming families this happens naturally; for office workers it may need a deliberate morning walk.
- **Be active during the day** — but finish vigorous exercise 2–3 hours before bed if it keeps you alert.
- **Limit naps** to 20–30 minutes, before mid-afternoon.
- **Avoid caffeine** (tea, coffee, colas) after early afternoon if sleep is a problem.

**Evening**
- **Eat dinner 2–3 hours before bed**, and keep it lighter. Late heavy meals worsen acidity, blood sugar and sleep.
- **Dim lights** in the last hour; avoid bright screens, or use night mode and lower brightness.
- **Avoid alcohol** as a sleep aid. It helps people fall asleep but fragments sleep later in the night and worsens snoring and apnoea.
- **Tobacco and nicotine** are stimulants that disturb sleep ([[16 - Tobacco, Alcohol & Environmental Exposures]]).
- **A wind-down routine:** reading, gentle stretching, prayer, slow breathing or a warm bath.

**Bedroom**
- **Dark:** curtains or an eye mask; no bright lights.
- **Quiet:** earplugs if needed.
- **Cool and well-ventilated.**
- **Mosquito protection** (nets, screens) — both for sleep and to prevent dengue and malaria ([[17 - Infections, Hygiene & Vaccines]]).
- **A night light** on the path to the toilet for older adults, to prevent falls ([[13 - Cardio, Mobility & Balance]]).

**Consistency**
- **Same wake-up time every day**, even after a bad night. This is the single strongest anchor for the body clock.

## 8. When to see a doctor about sleep

- Loud snoring with pauses in breathing or gasping
- Persistent daytime sleepiness, or falling asleep while driving or sitting
- Insomnia lasting more than a few weeks despite good habits
- Needing sleeping pills regularly
- Unusual movements or behaviour during sleep, such as acting out dreams (which can be an early sign of some neurological conditions in older adults)
- Waking at night breathless, or needing to sleep propped up on pillows (possible heart failure)

---

## The layer, in one line

Sleep is the body's nightly repair shift, timed by a light-driven internal clock; 7–9 regular hours protect blood sugar, appetite, blood pressure, immunity and the brain, and the main levers are morning light, regular timing, less caffeine, earlier lighter dinners, dark quiet bedrooms and treating snoring with pauses as a medical problem.
