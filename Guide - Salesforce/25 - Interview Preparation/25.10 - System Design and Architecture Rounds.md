# System Design and Architecture Rounds

## What is being assessed

Not whether you produce the right answer — there is rarely one. Whether you **structure the problem, surface the constraints, and defend your choices while remaining open to changing them.**

The strongest candidates spend the first ten minutes of an hour asking questions, and the interviewer learns more from those questions than from the design.

---

## The structure

```
1. Clarify          10 min   requirements, volume, users, constraints
2. Data model        10 min   objects, relationships, and why
3. Sharing            5 min   OWD and how access is granted
4. Automation        10 min   which mechanism where
5. Integration       10 min   patterns, direction, failure handling
6. Scale             10 min   volume, skew, archiving
7. Operations         5 min   monitoring, deployment, environments
```

**Announce the structure at the start.** "I'd like to clarify requirements first, then work through the data model, sharing, automation, integration, and scale — does that work?" It shows you have done this before and it stops the interviewer wondering where you are going.

---

## Clarifying

The questions that change the design, roughly in order of impact.

**Volume and distribution.** "How many records, and how are they distributed?" Two million claims evenly spread is a different design from two million with 60% under one account. **Asking about distribution rather than only count is a senior signal**, because skew causes more production failures than volume does.

**Users.** How many, what kind — internal, partner, customer — and on what device. The licence type decides the sharing model, and deciding it late is expensive.

**Latency.** What genuinely needs to be real time. Most stated real-time requirements are not, and asking frequently changes the answer.

**Existing systems.** What already exists that this should use rather than replace.

**Who maintains it.** A developer or an administrator. That decides flow versus Apex more than complexity does.

**Growth.** What this looks like in three years.

**Constraints.** Regulatory, data residency, existing contracts, team capability.

**Ask three or four, not all of them.** A candidate working through a checklist is as unconvincing as one who asks none.

## Data model

Draw the objects and relationships, and **say why for each relationship choice** — that is the part being assessed.

```
Account
  └── Asset (master-detail)
        ├── Machine_Coverage__c (junction, MD both sides)
        └── Warranty_Claim__c (master-detail)
              └── Claim_Part__c (master-detail)

Service_Contract__c (lookup to Account)
  └── Machine_Coverage__c

Claim_Archive__b (big object)
Machine_Reading__b (big object)
ErpInvoice__x (external object)
```

**The reasoning to voice:**

"Master-detail down the claim hierarchy, because at eight million claims the sharing consequence matters — a master-detail child generates no sharing rows of its own. The risk is lock contention on the asset from roll-up recalculation, which I'd accept if the asset distribution is even and reconsider if it is not."

"The junction is master-detail on both sides, and it needs a unique composite key field — a trigger-based duplicate check misses duplicates arriving together in the same insert."

"Telemetry in a big object, never a standard object. The index is defined at creation and cannot change, so how it will be queried decides that up front."

"Invoices as an external object. Twelve years of history that is never reported on in Salesforce does not need syncing, and the tradeoff — no reporting, no automation — is one the requirement can absorb."

**Two things that mark experience:** external IDs on every integrated object from day one, and a retention boundary decided before launch. Both are retrofits that become projects.

## Sharing

Short, and worth getting right because it is where scale problems originate.

**"What is the default, and how is access granted?"**

```
Account, Asset          Public Read Only
Warranty_Claim__c       inherited from Asset via master-detail
Service_Contract__c     Private, sharing rules by region to public groups
Partner users           Partner Community, account role hierarchy, sharing set
```

**The points to raise:**

"Sharing is materialized, not computed — every grant is a row. So hierarchy depth multiplies the sharing table, and I'd keep it as shallow as the access requirement allows."

"Groups rather than roles in sharing rules — cheaper to recalculate and clearer about intent."

"Integration users get no role, which removes their hierarchy sharing rows entirely. That is a one-field change and it prevents a real problem."

"Worth questioning whether private OWD is earning its cost. If most users can see most records anyway, Public Read Only with a few restrictions generates far fewer rows."

## Automation

**Per requirement, with the reason.**

```
Derived fields on save           before trigger — no second save
Cross-object validation          before trigger with addError
Roll-up beyond the native limit  after trigger, updating only changed parents
Approval routing                 approval process, dynamic approver by role
ERP notification                 platform event, subscriber calls out
Overnight reconciliation         scheduled Apex starting a batch
Screen-based claim submission    screen flow with invocable Apex
```

**The reasoning that matters:**

"One trigger per object, because order between triggers is undefined and that produces intermittent wrong behaviour that works in the sandbox."

"A platform event rather than a Queueable for the ERP notification, because three consumers eventually wanted it and adding a fourth requires no change to the publisher."

"A flow orchestrating invocable Apex for the submission — the administrator owns the process, the logic has tests, and the invocable receives every row at once so it stays bulk-safe."

"A bypass mechanism from day one. A hierarchy Custom Setting, tested, so automation can be disabled for a load or an incident without a deployment."

---

## Integration

**Per flow, not as one design.** Each has a different pattern.

```
Product catalogue, ERP → SF     nightly Bulk API upsert on external ID
Closed claims, SF → ERP         platform event, subscriber calls out, Finalizer retry
Invoice history                 external object, not synced
Telemetry                       filtered outside SF, platform events for faults only
```

**The reasoning:**

"Upsert on an external ID throughout, so a retry is safe. Without it a timeout that actually succeeded creates a duplicate on the next attempt, and nobody discovers it until reconciliation."

"The claim notification is enqueued rather than called, because a callout cannot follow DML."

"Telemetry filtered before it reaches Salesforce. Millions of readings would exhaust the event allocation and provide no value — only faults above a severity threshold arrive."

"A correlation Id on every callout and event, so a business operation can be traced across both systems' logs."

"And reconciliation per flow. Silent divergence is the failure mode nobody has a symptom for."

**Where I would question the requirement:** a real-time inventory lookup against a system whose 95th percentile response is eleven seconds. Syncing hourly with a manual refresh action is frequently what the business actually needs, and asking what latency is genuinely required changes the design.

## Scale

**The section that separates senior from mid.**

"At eight million claims, three things matter.

**Distribution** — I'd want to know the claims-per-asset and per-account distribution before finalizing the model, because skew causes more failures than volume.

**Retention** — a three-year boundary, with older claims archived to a big object and surfaced by a component on the asset. That keeps the queryable object at two million rather than eight, which changes what counts as a selective query and improves every report on it at once.

**Query selectivity** — every list view and report filter checked against the query plan. `Status != Closed` on an object where most claims are open cannot be optimized regardless of indexing, so filters need date bounds and positive value lists."

**Then the operational side:**

"Batch jobs staggered rather than all at 02:00, because only five run concurrently and the sixth ends up running during business hours."

"Scope size tuned per job, and the query locator ordered by parent so contention groups into single chunks."

"And I'd want batch job duration trended, because a job whose runtime has doubled over six months is telling you the data crossed a threshold — that is the earliest warning available and nobody watches it."

---

## Operations

Brief, and its absence is noticed.

"Source control with branches mapped to orgs, CI validating every pull request with `deploy validate`, and a delta manifest so only what changed deploys.

Post-deployment steps in a versioned Apex script — Custom Setting records, scheduled job rescheduling — because those do not deploy and the ones nobody documents are the ones that break production.

A custom logging framework publishing through platform events, so failures survive rollback and are queryable.

And monitoring that alerts on absence as well as failure. A job that stopped running produces no error record, and that is the failure mode most orgs have no coverage for."

## Handling pushback

Interviewers push to see whether you reason or defend.

> "Why not use a flow for the roll-up?"

"You could, and at low volume I would. At this volume a flow with a Get Records element runs per record, so a save of 200 claims issues 200 queries against a budget shared with everything else on the object. Apex collects and queries once. If the volume assumption is wrong — if this object saves a handful at a time — the flow is the better answer because an administrator maintains it."

> "That seems over-engineered."

"It might be. Which part are you thinking of?" — and then engage with it honestly. If the answer is the archiving, "you're right that at two million it can wait; I'd want the retention policy agreed now because retrofitting it onto a populated object is a project, but the implementation can be later."

> "What if the volume is a hundredth of that?"

"Then most of this changes. Lookup rather than master-detail, no archiving, a flow rather than Apex for the roll-up, and no big objects. The data model decisions were driven by the volume assumption, and if that is wrong the design should be simpler."

**Changing your answer when given new information is a strength, not a weakness.** Defending a design against a valid objection is the failure mode.

## What separates the levels

**Mid-level:** produces a workable design, answers the questions asked, knows the mechanisms.

**Senior:** asks about distribution and retention unprompted, names tradeoffs without being asked, raises operational concerns, and questions a requirement that deserves it.

**Architect:** thinks about the org rather than the feature — governance, who maintains it, how it evolves, what it constrains later. Comfortable saying "I'd want to understand why before building this."

**The specific things that read as experience:**

```
Asking about distribution, not just volume
A retention policy decided before launch
External IDs from day one
A tested bypass mechanism
Alerting on absence, not only on failure
Questioning a stated real-time requirement
Naming what the design gives up
```

**Each of those is learned by having got it wrong**, which is why they are hard to fake.

---

## Common mistakes

**Designing before clarifying.** The most damaging, and the most common.

**Ignoring volume.** A design with no stated assumption about scale cannot be evaluated.

**Everything in Apex.** Reaching for code where an approval process or a screen flow would do reads as unfamiliarity with the platform.

**No failure handling.** What happens when the ERP is down is part of the design, not an afterthought.

**No operational thinking.** How it is deployed, monitored, and maintained.

**Defending rather than reasoning.** The interviewer is testing how you think, and rigidity is the wrong signal.

**Silence.** Thinking without narrating gives the interviewer nothing to assess.

---

## Practising

**Take a domain you know and design it aloud, timed.** An hour, structured, out loud. It is uncomfortable and it is the only way to find out that knowing an answer and articulating it under time pressure are different skills.

**Three scenarios is enough** to cover the shapes: a transactional system at volume, an integration-heavy design, and a user-facing process with a portal.

**Record yourself once.** Most people discover they skip the clarification, under-explain their reasoning, and talk too fast.
