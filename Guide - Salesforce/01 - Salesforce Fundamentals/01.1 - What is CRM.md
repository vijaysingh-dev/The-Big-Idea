# What is CRM

## The problem CRM solves

Every company that sells to other companies asks the same questions daily:

- Who are we talking to right now, and how far along is each conversation?
- Who spoke to them last, and what did we promise?
- How much money will realistically close this quarter?
- When a customer complains, does the person answering the phone know anything about them?

In a five-person company, the answers live in people's heads and a spreadsheet. At fifty people the spreadsheet starts lying. At five hundred people across three countries, with reps joining and leaving, not knowing costs you deals and customers.

**CRM (Customer Relationship Management)** is the software category that answers these questions. The key idea: it stores the *relationship*, not just the transactions.

An order system knows a customer bought something. A CRM knows who introduced them, which competitor they looked at, that they are annoyed about a support ticket from last month, and that their contract renews in ninety days.

This matters to you as a developer because it explains why Salesforce's data model looks the way it does. You are modelling a relationship over time, not a set of transactions.

## What a CRM stores

Nearly every CRM converges on the same core entities. Learn them as ideas first and the Salesforce objects become obvious.

| Idea | What it represents | Salesforce object |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Company | An organization you sell to or support | Account |
| Person | An individual at that company | Contact |
| Unqualified interest | Someone who might become a customer | Lead |
| Potential revenue | A specific deal in progress | Opportunity |
| Interaction | A call, email, meeting, or task | Activity (Task / Event) |
| Problem | A customer issue needing resolution | Case |
| Outreach | A marketing program that drives interest | Campaign |

Two details cause a lot of confusion later, so get them straight now.

**A Lead is a holding pen.** A Lead is deliberately *not* connected to an Account or Contact. It is one flat record holding a name, an email, and a company name typed as plain text. It exists so unverified data does not pollute your clean customer database. When the lead is qualified, *lead conversion* turns that one record into up to three records: an Account, a Contact, and usually an Opportunity. Full detail in [[04.4 - Lead Conversion]].

**Opportunity is where the money lives.** Forecasting, sales commission, pipeline reports, and most executive dashboards all read from Opportunity. That is why Opportunity attracts more custom automation and more validation rules than any other object, and why changing its stage list is a political event rather than a technical one.

Here is how those objects connect in a query. This is SOQL, Salesforce's query language, covered properly in section 08 — read it now just to see the shape of the model:

```sql
SELECT Name, Industry,
       (SELECT FirstName, LastName, Email FROM Contacts),
       (SELECT Name, Amount, StageName, CloseDate FROM Opportunities
        WHERE IsClosed = false),
       (SELECT CaseNumber, Subject, Status FROM Cases
        WHERE IsClosed = false)
FROM Account
WHERE Id = :accountId
```

One Account, with its people, its open deals, and its open complaints, in a single query. `Contacts`, `Opportunities`, and `Cases` are child relationships that Salesforce gives you out of the box. You did not build any of it.

## The three halves of CRM

Salesforce sells these as separate products, which explains how this course is weighted.

**Sales.** Managing the pipeline from first interest to closed deal. Leads, opportunities, quotes, forecasts, territories. In Salesforce this is *Sales Cloud*, section 04.

**Service.** Managing the relationship after the sale, when something breaks or the customer needs help. Cases, service levels, knowledge bases, call routing. In Salesforce this is *Service Cloud*, section 05.

**Marketing.** Creating and warming up interest before sales gets involved. Email campaigns, journeys, segmentation. In Salesforce this is *Marketing Cloud* or *Account Engagement*. It is effectively a separate platform with its own data model and its own skill set, so it sits outside this course except where it hands leads to Sales Cloud.

Sales Cloud and Service Cloud share one database, the same Account and Contact records, and the same automation engine. That is the whole commercial argument for Salesforce: the support agent taking an angry call can see the deal a sales rep is trying to close with that same company, because it is literally the same Account record.

## How this plays out in a real org

Textbook definitions stop above this line. What follows is what actually decides whether an implementation succeeds, and where you will spend your time.

**A CRM becomes load-bearing fast.** Once Opportunity is the official source of revenue numbers, other systems start depending on it — the data warehouse, the commission tool, the board deck. So schema changes on core objects are never local. Treat a field on Opportunity the way a backend engineer treats a public API: adding is cheap, renaming or deleting is expensive and needs coordination.

**Adoption fails before technology does.** The most common way a CRM project dies is that reps stop entering data, because entering it takes time and only helps their manager. This has a direct engineering consequence: every validation rule, required field, and approval step is a tax on the person typing. Experienced Salesforce engineers push back on requirements that add friction without adding value, and try to fill fields through defaults, automation, or integration instead of asking a human. "Can we derive this instead of requiring it?" is one of the most valuable questions you can ask in a requirements meeting.

**Data model requests are often business arguments in disguise.** When someone asks for a new value in Opportunity Stage, they are proposing a change to how the company defines a sale. When two business units want different stage lists on the same object, you are being asked to settle an internal disagreement using configuration. Record Types exist for exactly this ([[02.11 - Record Types and Page Layouts]]), but reaching for them too early creates complexity you never remove. Telling apart technical requests and political ones is a big part of seniority here.

**Duplicates never fully go away.** The same company arrives through a web form, a trade show list, a marketing import, and a rep typing it manually, spelled four different ways. Every real org spends ongoing effort on matching rules, duplicate rules, and dedupe at the integration layer. This is why *external IDs* — a field marked unique that holds the record's identifier from an outside system — show up so early in integration design. See [[10.3 - Upsert, Merge and External IDs]].

**Reporting drives schema more than you expect.** A field that exists only so a manager can group a report is still a real requirement. But the opposite trap is worse: teams add a custom field for every reporting need until the object has three hundred fields, page layouts are unusable, and queries start hitting row-size limits. Check whether an existing field, a formula field, or a report-level grouping can answer the question before adding storage.

## One customer, end to end

Trace a single customer through a real business-to-business org so the objects connect to something concrete.

1. Someone at Northwind Manufacturing downloads a whitepaper from your site. A **Lead** is created with their name, email, and company name as text. Nothing is linked yet.
2. Assignment rules route the Lead to the rep covering manufacturing in western India. See [[04.3 - Lead Assignment Rules and Queues]].
3. The rep calls, confirms there is a budget and a real need, and converts the Lead. That creates an **Account** for Northwind, a **Contact** for the person, and an **Opportunity** at stage "Qualification".
4. Over eight weeks the rep logs calls and meetings as **Activities** on the Opportunity. The deal moves through stages. A discount above twenty percent triggers an **Approval Process** routed to the regional director. See [[04.16 - Approval Processes in the Sales Cycle]].
5. The deal closes. The Opportunity is marked Closed Won, which feeds the quarterly forecast and, through an integration, creates an order in the ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning — the system running finance, inventory, and fulfilment).
6. Four months later the product stops working. The customer emails support. **Email-to-Case** creates a **Case** linked to the same Account and Contact. See [[05.6 - Email-to-Case and Web-to-Case]].
7. The Case misses its service level target, an **Escalation Rule** fires, and the account executive is notified, because the renewal is two months away and an unhappy customer is a churn risk.

Steps 1 to 5 are Sales Cloud. Steps 6 and 7 are Service Cloud. Same Account record throughout. That continuity is the product thesis.

Step 7 in code looks roughly like this. Do not worry about the syntax yet, only the idea that Service data and Sales data sit in the same query:

```java
List<Case> breachedCases = [
    SELECT Id, Subject, Account.Name, Account.OwnerId
    FROM Case
    WHERE IsClosed = false
      AND IsEscalated = true
      AND Account.Id IN (
          SELECT AccountId FROM Opportunity
          WHERE StageName = 'Closed Won' AND CloseDate = LAST_N_MONTHS:12
      )
];
```

"Show me escalated support tickets belonging to customers who bought from us in the last year." No integration, no join across systems, no nightly sync. That query is the reason companies buy both clouds together.

## Things to unlearn early

- **CRM means sales software.** Service and field service implementations are often bigger and more complex than sales.
- **The CRM should store everything about the customer.** It should store what you need to manage the relationship. Transaction-level detail belongs in the ERP or the data warehouse and gets surfaced through integration or external objects. Orgs that ignore this hit storage limits and slow queries. See [[02.14 - External Objects and Salesforce Connect]].
- **Standard objects are for beginners; real work uses custom objects.** Backwards. Standard objects come with reporting, forecasting, mobile support, Einstein features, and sharing behaviour you would otherwise build yourself. Rebuilding Opportunity as a custom object is a classic expensive mistake.
- **Configuration is a lesser skill than code.** On this platform, choosing configuration where it fits is a sign of experience. Code is something you then have to test, deploy, and maintain forever. See [[07.1 - Automation Landscape and Tool Selection]].
