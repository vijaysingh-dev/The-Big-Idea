# Fast Killers: Emergencies & Warning Signs

*Health & Fitness · 2026-10-30*

Some conditions kill in minutes or hours, and the outcome depends less on the hospital than on what happens in the first hour at home: whether someone recognises the signs, calls for help, does the right thing and doesn't waste time on home remedies. Every family should know these signs the way they know their phone number.

> This note is general education, not a substitute for emergency medical care. When in doubt, go to hospital.

---

## 0. Before anything happens: be prepared

- **Emergency numbers (India):** **112** (all emergencies), **108** (ambulance in most states), **102** (maternal and child transport in many states). Save them on every phone, including the local ambulance and nearest 24-hour hospital.
- **Know your nearest hospital** that has a **24-hour emergency department, ECG, CT scan** and, ideally, a **cath lab** (for heart attacks). Find it before you need it.
- **Keep a health card** for each family member: conditions, medicines, allergies, blood group ([[24 - Silent Killers - Diseases That Hide Until Late]]).
- **A basic home kit:** BP monitor, glucometer, thermometer, pulse oximeter, ORS sachets, paracetamol, a first-aid kit, and any prescribed emergency medicines.
- **Learn hands-only CPR** (section 2).

## 1. Heart attack

**Signs:**
- Chest **pain, pressure, tightness, squeezing or heaviness**, often in the centre of the chest, lasting more than a few minutes or coming and going
- Pain spreading to the **arm (often left), jaw, neck, back or upper stomach**
- **Breathlessness**, **sweating**, nausea or vomiting, light-headedness, sudden extreme tiredness
- **Women, older adults and people with diabetes** often have less typical symptoms: breathlessness, unusual fatigue, nausea, "gas" or indigestion-like discomfort, back or jaw pain, without classic chest pain.

**What to do:**
1. **Call 112/108** or get to the nearest hospital with ECG facilities **immediately**. Don't drive yourself if you can avoid it.
2. **Stop all activity**; sit or lie in a comfortable position.
3. **Aspirin:** if the person isn't allergic to aspirin, has no recent bleeding, and isn't on a blood thinner, emergency services commonly advise **chewing one regular-dose aspirin** (300–325 mg) while waiting for help. Follow the advice of the emergency operator or doctor.
4. Take prescribed **nitroglycerin/sorbitrate** under the tongue only if it has been prescribed for this.
5. **Note the time** symptoms started.
6. If the person becomes unresponsive and isn't breathing normally, **start CPR**.

**Don't:** take antacids and wait, assume it's "gas," wait to see if it passes, or delay for a family consultation. Every 30 minutes of delay costs heart muscle. Treatment to open the artery (angioplasty or clot-dissolving drugs) works best within the first hours — ideally within about 90–120 minutes of reaching care.

## 2. Cardiac arrest and CPR

**Cardiac arrest** is when the heart suddenly stops pumping effectively: the person collapses, is unresponsive, and isn't breathing normally (or only gasping). Without help, death follows within minutes.

**Hands-only CPR** (cardiopulmonary resuscitation):
1. **Check:** tap and shout. Unresponsive and not breathing normally?
2. **Call 112/108** (or get someone else to) and put the phone on speaker.
3. **Push hard and fast** in the **centre of the chest**: heel of one hand on the lower half of the breastbone, other hand on top, arms straight. Press down about **5–6 cm** at **100–120 compressions per minute** (the beat of the song "Stayin' Alive"), letting the chest rise fully between pushes.
4. **Don't stop** until help arrives, the person starts breathing normally, or an **AED** (automated external defibrillator, a device that shocks the heart back into rhythm, increasingly found in airports, malls and offices) is brought. Turn on the AED and follow its spoken instructions.

Doing CPR, even imperfectly, can double or triple survival. A short free course, often offered by hospitals and the Red Cross, is worth an afternoon.

## 3. Stroke

**BE FAST:**
- **B**alance: sudden loss of balance or coordination
- **E**yes: sudden vision loss or double vision
- **F**ace: one side drooping
- **A**rm (or leg): sudden weakness or numbness on one side
- **S**peech: slurred, confused or unable to speak or understand
- **T**ime: call 112/108 and go to a hospital **with a CT scanner**

Also: **sudden severe headache** ("the worst ever"), sudden confusion.

**What to do:** note the **time symptoms started** (or when the person was last seen normal); keep them lying on their side if drowsy or vomiting; **give nothing by mouth** (not water, not tablets, not food), since swallowing may be affected. **Don't give aspirin** for a suspected stroke at home, because a bleeding stroke can't be told apart from a clot without a CT scan. Clot treatments work only within hours ([[20 - Blood Pressure, Stroke & Kidney Disease]]).

A **TIA** (mini-stroke) that gets better within minutes is still an emergency.

## 4. Severe infection and sepsis

**Sepsis** is the body's overwhelming response to an infection, damaging its own organs. It can follow pneumonia, urinary infections, skin and wound infections, and abdominal infections. People with diabetes, older adults, the very young, and anyone with a weak immune system are at higher risk.

**Warning signs** of a severe infection:
- Fever **or** unusually low temperature, with shivering
- **Confusion**, drowsiness or slurred speech
- **Fast breathing** or breathlessness
- **Fast heart rate**, low BP, dizziness on standing
- **Cold, clammy, pale or mottled skin**
- **Very little urine**
- Feeling "like I might die"

**Go to hospital immediately.** Sepsis needs antibiotics and fluids within hours. In older adults, sudden confusion or not getting out of bed may be the only sign of a serious infection.

## 5. Dengue danger signs

Most dengue is managed at home with rest, fluids and paracetamol. But severe dengue can develop, often around **days 3–7, as the fever comes down**. Go to hospital immediately with any of these **warning signs**:

- **Severe abdominal pain** or tenderness
- **Persistent vomiting**
- **Bleeding**: from gums, nose, in vomit or stool (black stool), or red spots under the skin
- **Restlessness, drowsiness or confusion**
- **Cold, clammy skin**, dizziness, fainting
- **Very little urine** (less than every 6–8 hours)
- **Breathlessness**
- **Rapid rise in haematocrit with a rapid fall in platelets** on blood tests

**Do:** drink plenty of fluids (water, ORS, coconut water, soups), use paracetamol for fever, and get a platelet count and haematocrit checked as advised. **Don't:** take aspirin or NSAIDs (ibuprofen, diclofenac); rely on papaya leaf extract instead of medical care; or rush for platelet transfusions without a doctor's decision (most patients don't need them) ([[17 - Infections, Hygiene & Vaccines]]).

## 6. Snakebite

India has the world's highest number of snakebite deaths, mostly in rural farming families, often at night or in fields.

**Do:**
1. **Stay calm** and move away from the snake. Don't try to catch or kill it; a photo from a safe distance helps.
2. **Keep the bitten limb still** and at or below heart level; the person should lie still and avoid walking if possible.
3. **Remove rings, bangles, watches and tight clothing** near the bite before swelling starts.
4. **Get to a hospital immediately** — ideally one that stocks **antivenom** (anti-snake venom, ASV, available free at government hospitals). Call 108.

**Don't:** cut the wound, suck out venom, apply a tight tourniquet, apply ice, chemicals or herbs, or go to a traditional healer first. These waste time and cause harm. Some bites (from kraits, which often bite at night while people sleep on the floor) cause little pain but lead to paralysis hours later — drowsiness, drooping eyelids, difficulty swallowing or breathing. **Every snakebite needs hospital assessment**, even if it looks minor. Sleeping on a raised bed under a mosquito net, and using a torch and footwear at night, prevents many bites.

## 7. Heat stroke

Heat waves are increasingly dangerous in India, especially for older adults, outdoor workers, people with heart, kidney or mental health conditions, and those on some medicines (diuretics, some BP and psychiatric drugs).

**Heat exhaustion:** heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, headache, nausea, cramps. Move to a cool place, drink water or ORS, loosen clothing, rest.

**Heat stroke (an emergency):**
- Very high body temperature
- **Confusion**, slurred speech, agitation, fainting or seizures
- Hot, dry or flushed skin (sweating may have stopped)
- Fast heartbeat

**What to do:** call 112/108, move the person to shade or a cool room, and **cool them fast**: remove excess clothing, pour or sponge cool water over the body while fanning, apply cold packs to the neck, armpits and groin. Give sips of water **only if fully conscious**.

**Prevention:** avoid heavy outdoor work during the hottest hours (roughly noon to 4 pm), drink water regularly without waiting for thirst, wear loose light clothing and a head covering, take breaks in shade, and check on elderly neighbours during heat waves.

## 8. Blood sugar emergencies (diabetes)

**Low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia)**, mainly in people on insulin or sulfonylurea tablets (such as glimepiride, gliclazide):
- Shaking, sweating, hunger, palpitations, confusion, unusual behaviour, drowsiness, seizures
- **Act:** if conscious, give **15–20 g of fast sugar** — 3–4 teaspoons of sugar or glucose in water, or a glass of juice — then recheck after 15 minutes and repeat if still low; follow with a snack. If unconscious, **don't put anything in the mouth**; call 112/108.

**Very high blood sugar:** extreme thirst, frequent urination, vomiting, abdominal pain, fruity-smelling breath, deep fast breathing, drowsiness — possible **diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)** or a severe high-sugar state. Go to hospital.

## 9. Breathing emergencies

- **Choking:** if the person can't speak, cough or breathe, give up to **5 firm back blows** between the shoulder blades, then up to **5 abdominal thrusts** (the Heimlich manoeuvre: stand behind, fist just above the navel, pull sharply inwards and upwards). Alternate until the object comes out or the person becomes unresponsive, then start CPR and call for help.
- **Severe asthma or COPD attack:** unable to speak in full sentences, lips turning blue, inhaler not helping — go to hospital.
- **Anaphylaxis** (severe allergic reaction, for example to a bee sting, food or medicine): swelling of lips, tongue or throat, difficulty breathing, wheezing, a sudden rash with dizziness or fainting. Call 112/108; use an **adrenaline auto-injector** if one has been prescribed.

## 10. Poisoning

**Pesticide poisoning** (accidental or intentional) is a major cause of death in rural India.
- **Go to hospital immediately** and **take the container or label** with you.
- **Don't induce vomiting** or give milk, salt water or home remedies.
- Remove contaminated clothing; wash skin with soap and water.

**Medicine overdose** (including paracetamol, which can cause liver failure with few early symptoms), **kerosene**, **household chemicals**, **methanol** (from illicit liquor; causes vomiting, blurred vision and breathing problems), and **carbon monoxide** (from heaters or chulhas in closed rooms; causes headache, dizziness and drowsiness in several people at once): all need urgent hospital care.

If someone has harmed themselves intentionally, also see [[15 - Stress, Mental Health & Social Connection]] for support and Tele-MANAS (14416).

## 11. Injuries

- **Road accidents:** don't move someone with a possible neck or back injury unless they're in danger; call 108; control bleeding with firm pressure. India's Good Samaritan law protects bystanders who help accident victims from harassment.
- **Severe bleeding:** press firmly on the wound with a clean cloth and keep pressing.
- **Head injury** with vomiting, worsening headache, drowsiness, confusion, or in someone on blood thinners: go to hospital.
- **Burns:** cool under running water for 20 minutes; don't apply toothpaste, ghee, oil or ice; cover loosely with clean cling film or cloth; seek care for large, deep, facial or electrical burns.
- **Dog or animal bites:** wash with soap and water for 15 minutes; rabies vaccination the same day ([[17 - Infections, Hygiene & Vaccines]]).
- **Drowning:** call for help; once the person is out of the water, check breathing; start CPR if needed.
- **Hip fracture after a fall:** don't try to walk; call for help ([[21 - Joints & Bones - Osteoarthritis, Osteoporosis & Falls]]).

## 12. Other "go now" signs

- Sudden **severe abdominal pain**, or a hard, rigid abdomen
- **Vomiting blood** or passing black, tarry stools
- **Sudden breathlessness**, especially with chest pain or a swollen calf (possible clot in the lungs)
- **A seizure** lasting more than 5 minutes, or a first-ever seizure
- **Sudden confusion** or loss of consciousness
- **Severe pain or bleeding in pregnancy**, reduced baby movements, severe headache with swelling and visual changes in pregnancy
- **A child who is unusually floppy, drowsy, breathing fast, refusing feeds, or with a non-fading rash**
- **High fever with a stiff neck**, rash or confusion

---

## The layer, in one line

Heart attacks, cardiac arrest, stroke, sepsis, severe dengue, snakebite, heat stroke, sugar emergencies, choking, anaphylaxis and poisoning kill within minutes to hours, and survival depends on recognising the signs, calling 112/108, doing the few right first-aid steps (CPR, aspirin for heart attack when advised, cooling, keeping a bitten limb still) and avoiding home remedies that waste time.
