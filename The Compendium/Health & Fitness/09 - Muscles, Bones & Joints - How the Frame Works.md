# Muscles, Bones & Joints: How the Frame Works

*Health & Fitness · 2026-10-30*

We tend to think of bones as dead scaffolding and joints as hinges that simply wear out with age. Both are living tissue, constantly broken down and rebuilt in response to how they're used. That's why "rest it" is often the wrong advice for an ageing body, and why the frame you'll have at 70 is being built by what you do at 30, 50 and 60.

---

## 1. Muscle: how it's built and how it contracts

There are three kinds of muscle: **skeletal muscle** (attached to bones, under voluntary control), **cardiac muscle** (the heart, [[05 - Blood, Circulation & the Heart]]) and **smooth muscle** (in the walls of the gut, blood vessels and bladder). This note is about skeletal muscle.

A muscle is a bundle of long cells called **muscle fibres**, each packed with protein filaments (**actin** and **myosin**) that slide past each other when the muscle contracts. A nerve signal from the brain triggers the contraction, which is powered by ATP ([[03 - Energy Metabolism - How the Body Makes and Uses Fuel]]).

**Fibre types:**
- **Slow-twitch (type I)** fibres are endurance fibres: rich in mitochondria, slow to tire, used for posture, walking and long efforts.
- **Fast-twitch (type II)** fibres are power fibres: strong and fast but tire quickly, used for lifting, climbing stairs and catching yourself when you trip.

Fast-twitch fibres are lost faster with age and inactivity. This is a big reason older adults struggle to get up from a chair or recover from a stumble, and why strength training matters more than walking alone for preventing falls ([[12 - Strength Training - Muscle as Medicine]]).

### How muscle grows
When muscle is worked against a challenging load, it signals a process called **muscle protein synthesis** — building new protein into the fibres. Over weeks of repeated training, fibres get thicker and stronger. Muscle is constantly balancing building (synthesis) and breaking down (breakdown):
- **Training and protein** tip the balance towards building.
- **Inactivity, illness, low protein, high cortisol and ageing** tip it towards breakdown.

Older muscle has **anabolic resistance** — it responds less strongly to the same amount of protein and exercise. The practical answer isn't to give up but to train regularly and eat a bit more protein per meal ([[11 - The Indian Plate - Rebuilding the Thali]]).

## 2. Tendons and ligaments

- **Tendons** connect muscle to bone, transmitting the pull of the muscle.
- **Ligaments** connect bone to bone, stabilising joints.

Both are made mostly of **collagen**, a tough, rope-like protein. They have limited blood supply, so they adapt to training more slowly than muscle (months rather than weeks) and heal slowly when injured. This is why increasing exercise gradually matters, especially for older adults returning to activity: muscles get stronger faster than tendons can keep up with.

## 3. Bone: living, remodelling tissue

Bone is a composite of **collagen** (which gives flexibility) and **calcium phosphate minerals** (which give hardness), plus living cells. It stores about 99% of the body's calcium and houses the **bone marrow**, where blood cells are made.

Bone is constantly **remodelled**:
- **Osteoclasts** break down old bone.
- **Osteoblasts** build new bone.

Roughly a tenth of the adult skeleton is replaced each year. Whether bone gets denser or thinner depends on the balance between the two.

### What builds and keeps bone
- **Load.** Bone responds to mechanical stress — muscles pulling and body weight pressing on it. Weight-bearing activity (walking, stair climbing, carrying) and especially strength training signal bone to stay dense. Astronauts and bedridden patients lose bone quickly without load.
- **Calcium**, from dairy, ragi, sesame, green leafy vegetables, tofu and small fish eaten with bones ([[02 - Micronutrients & RDAs]]).
- **Vitamin D**, needed to absorb calcium from the gut. Deficiency is very common in India, partly due to indoor lifestyles, skin covering, darker skin (which needs more sun to make vitamin D) and pollution blocking UVB light.
- **Protein**, since about half of bone volume is protein.
- **Hormones:** oestrogen and testosterone protect bone. The fall in oestrogen at menopause speeds bone loss sharply for several years ([[04 - Hormones - The Body's Messaging System]]).

### Peak bone mass
Bone density peaks around the late 20s to early 30s, then slowly declines. The higher the peak, the longer it takes to reach dangerously low levels. Children and young adults who play sports, eat enough calcium and protein and get sunlight build a bigger "bone bank" for later life ([[29 - Health Across Life Stages]]).

### What weakens bone
Inactivity, low calcium or vitamin D, low body weight, smoking and tobacco, heavy alcohol, long-term steroid medicines, untreated thyroid overactivity, some acid-suppressing medicines used long term, and early menopause.

**Osteoporosis** is bone thinned to the point that it breaks easily — a hip, spine or wrist fracture from a minor fall. It's silent until the first fracture. [[21 - Joints & Bones - Osteoarthritis, Osteoporosis & Falls]] covers it in detail.

## 4. Joints: where bones meet

A **joint** is where two bones meet. The ones that matter most for daily life — knees, hips, shoulders — are **synovial joints**:

- The ends of the bones are covered with **articular cartilage**, a smooth, slippery, rubbery layer that lets bones glide with very little friction.
- The joint is enclosed in a **capsule** lined by a membrane that makes **synovial fluid**, a thick lubricant like egg white.
- **Ligaments** hold the joint together.
- The knee also has two **menisci**, C-shaped cartilage pads that act as shock absorbers.

### Cartilage needs movement
Cartilage has **no blood supply**. It gets its nutrients by being compressed and released, like a sponge squeezed and soaked. Regular, moderate movement keeps it nourished. Prolonged immobility actually harms cartilage. This is why "rest the knee completely" usually makes joint pain worse over time, while appropriate, gradual exercise helps.

### Muscles protect joints
Muscles act as the joint's active shock absorbers and stabilisers. Weak thigh muscles (**quadriceps**) mean more force goes straight through the knee cartilage with each step. Strengthening the quadriceps and hip muscles is one of the most effective treatments for knee pain ([[12 - Strength Training - Muscle as Medicine]]).

### Why knees hurt
Common causes of knee pain in middle and older age:
- **Osteoarthritis** — gradual breakdown of cartilage with inflammation of the whole joint. Most common in women over 50, in people who are overweight, and after past knee injuries. Indian lifestyles that involve squatting and sitting cross-legged on the floor for long periods, plus years of physical labour, can contribute.
- **Weak muscles and poor alignment**, such as flat feet, knock-knees or bow legs, which load one side of the knee more.
- **Injuries** to ligaments or menisci.
- **Inflammatory arthritis** (such as rheumatoid arthritis, an autoimmune disease, [[06 - The Immune System & Inflammation]]) — usually affects several joints, with morning stiffness lasting more than 30 minutes and swelling.
- **Gout** — sudden, intensely painful swelling, often in the big toe, linked to high uric acid.

## 5. The frame and body weight

Each kilo of body weight adds several kilos of force across the knee with every step, more on stairs and when squatting. Even modest weight loss in overweight people noticeably reduces knee pain and slows osteoarthritis.

But weight loss without strength training risks losing muscle and bone along with fat — making the frame weaker, not stronger. Weight loss for joint health should always pair a moderate calorie deficit with enough protein and strength work.

## 6. Posture, flexibility and mobility

- **Mobility** is the ability to move a joint through its full range of motion with control. It's lost gradually when ranges aren't used: if you never squat, you lose the ability to squat.
- **Flexibility** is how far a muscle can lengthen. Stiff hips and hamstrings change how load passes through the back and knees.
- **Posture** matters less than once thought; there's no single "perfect" posture. Varying positions and avoiding long static periods (hours of sitting or standing in one position) matters more.

Daily gentle movement through full ranges — rotating the shoulders, bending, gentle squats to a comfortable depth — maintains mobility at any age ([[13 - Cardio, Mobility & Balance]]).

## 7. Signs the frame needs attention

- Joint pain lasting more than a few weeks, or that wakes you at night
- A joint that's swollen, hot or red
- Morning stiffness lasting more than 30 minutes (suggests inflammatory arthritis)
- Loss of height, a stooped back, or new back pain in older adults (possible spine fractures from osteoporosis)
- A fracture from a minor fall
- Difficulty getting up from a chair without using hands
- Unexplained bone pain

---

## The layer, in one line

Muscle, bone, cartilage and tendons are all living tissues rebuilt according to how they're used: load and protein build them, inactivity and age thin them, cartilage needs movement to stay nourished, and strong muscles are the joints' best shock absorbers.
