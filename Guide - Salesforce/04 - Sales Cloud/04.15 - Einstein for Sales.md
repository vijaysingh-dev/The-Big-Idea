# Einstein for Sales

## What Einstein is in this context

Einstein is the umbrella name for predictive and generative features built into the clouds. In Sales Cloud the predictive ones are the established, well-understood part; the generative ones are newer and covered more fully in section 24.

The framing that matters for a developer: **these are models trained on your org's own history.** They are not general-purpose intelligence applied to your data. They learn from what your org has actually done, which means their quality is bounded by the quality and volume of your historical data.

## Einstein Lead Scoring

Analyses which leads converted historically, and scores current leads on likelihood to convert. It also surfaces the factors driving each score — "leads from trade shows in manufacturing convert at three times the average" — which is often more useful than the score itself.

Requirements to work at all:

- A meaningful volume of historical leads, typically in the low thousands
- A reasonable mix of converted and unconverted outcomes
- **Honest disqualification.** This is the one orgs fail. If leads are never marked Disqualified and instead sit in Working forever, the model has no negative examples to learn from, and the scores are close to meaningless.

The score appears as a field and can be used in list views, reports, and assignment rules — routing high-scoring leads to senior reps, for example.

## Einstein Opportunity Scoring

The same idea applied to open deals: likelihood to close, based on the org's history of won and lost opportunities.

Signals it uses include stage history, activity volume and recency, deal size relative to typical, age, and close-date movement. The factors it reports are frequently the useful output — a deal flagged because its close date has moved four times is telling a manager something specific.

It works best where deal volume is high enough to learn from. An org closing thirty deals a year will not get much from it.

## Einstein Forecasting

Produces a predicted forecast number independent of what reps entered, based on historical pipeline behaviour and conversion patterns.

Its value is not replacing the rep forecast but **contrasting with it**. A rep committing to 40 lakh while the model predicts 25 lakh is a conversation worth having, and the gap is more informative than either number alone.

## Einstein Activity Capture and Insights

Activity Capture logs email and calendar interactions automatically ([[04.14 - Path, Kanban and Sales Productivity]]). Built on it, Einstein produces insights: a key contact has gone quiet, a competitor was mentioned in an email thread, a deal has had no engagement in three weeks.

The reporting caveats from 04.14 apply — captured activity is not fully available to standard reporting or SOQL, which constrains what you can build on top of it.

## Conversation Insights

Analyses recorded sales calls, transcribing them and detecting mentions of competitors, pricing, and defined keywords. Useful for coaching and for spotting patterns across many calls.

Practical considerations before adopting: call recording has legal consent requirements that vary by jurisdiction, and India, the EU, and various US states differ meaningfully. This is a legal question before it is a technical one.

## Einstein Prediction Builder

The one a developer is most likely to actually build with. It creates a custom prediction on any object, without code.

You define: the object, the field to predict (a checkbox or a number), the segment of records to consider, and the fields the model may use. Salesforce trains a model and writes the prediction into a field.

Concrete uses in our manufacturing example:

- Predict which machines are likely to generate a warranty claim in the next quarter
- Predict which service contracts are at risk of non-renewal
- Predict which accounts are likely to expand

The output is a field like any other, so it can drive list views, reports, assignment, and automation:

```java
List<Service_Contract__c> atRisk = [
    SELECT Id, Name, Account__c, Renewal_Risk_Score__c
    FROM Service_Contract__c
    WHERE Status__c = 'Active'
      AND End_Date__c = NEXT_N_DAYS:180
      AND Renewal_Risk_Score__c > 70
    ORDER BY Annual_Value__c DESC
];
```

Requirements: a few hundred records minimum with known outcomes, and a reasonable balance between positive and negative examples. A prediction where 99 percent of records are one class produces a model that is accurate and useless.

## The generative features

**Einstein Sales Emails** drafts emails grounded in CRM record data — an outreach email referencing the account's industry and the opportunity's stage.

**Call summaries** generate a written summary from a recorded call.

**Prompt Builder** creates reusable prompt templates grounded in record data, which is the extensibility point for building your own generative features. See [[24.3 - Prompt Builder and Prompt Templates]].

**Agentforce** builds agents that can take actions rather than only produce text. See [[24.4 - Agentforce Fundamentals]].

The **Einstein Trust Layer** sits between the platform and the underlying model, handling data masking, grounding, and zero-retention with the model provider. This is what makes the features acceptable to enterprise security review, and understanding it is usually necessary to get a generative feature approved internally. See [[24.5 - Einstein Trust Layer]].

## What a developer should actually think about

**Data quality is the whole game.** A model trained on a pipeline where nobody updates close dates and nothing is ever marked lost learns from noise. Improving data hygiene improves model output more than any configuration.

**Predictions are probabilistic and should be presented as such.** A score of 80 does not mean the deal will close. Building automation that hard-branches on a prediction — auto-closing low-scoring opportunities, for instance — turns a useful signal into a self-fulfilling one.

**Check for bias in the training data.** A model trained on historical outcomes reproduces historical patterns, including ones the business would not endorse. If a particular segment was historically under-served, the model learns to score it lower, which reinforces the neglect. This is worth raising explicitly during design, not after.

**Predictions add fields and automation triggers.** A prediction field updating causes a record update, which fires triggers and flows. On a high-volume object this is real load, and it is easy to overlook because nobody typed the update.

**Feature availability varies by edition and add-on licensing.** Confirm what the org actually has before designing around it. See [[01.4 - Salesforce Editions and Licensing]].

## Building your own, without Einstein

Sometimes the requirement is a scoring model and Einstein is not licensed or not suitable. The honest options:

**A formula or Apex scoring model** using explicit business rules. Crude, fully transparent, and easy to explain to a sales leader — which matters more than accuracy in some organizations.

**An external model** called through an integration. A model trained in Python, deployed on a platform outside Salesforce, called from Apex or invoked through middleware, with the score written back to a field. See [[13.14 - Apex Callouts]].

The second is common in organizations with a data science function, and the integration pattern is straightforward: an asynchronous scoring job rather than a synchronous callout, so the model's latency does not sit inside a user's save.

```java
public with sharing class ExternalScoringService implements Queueable, Database.AllowsCallouts {

    private final Set<Id> opportunityIds;

    public ExternalScoringService(Set<Id> opportunityIds) {
        this.opportunityIds = opportunityIds;
    }

    public void execute(QueueableContext context) {
        List<Opportunity> opps = [
            SELECT Id, Amount, StageName, Age_Days__c, Activity_Count__c, Segment__c
            FROM Opportunity WHERE Id IN :opportunityIds
        ];

        HttpRequest req = new HttpRequest();
        req.setEndpoint('callout:Scoring_Service/predict');
        req.setMethod('POST');
        req.setHeader('Content-Type', 'application/json');
        req.setBody(JSON.serialize(ScoringPayload.from(opps)));

        HttpResponse res = new Http().send(req);
        if (res.getStatusCode() != 200) {
            throw new CalloutException('Scoring failed: ' + res.getStatusCode());
        }

        update ScoringPayload.applyScores(opps, res.getBody());
    }
}
```

## Practical guidance

**Fix data hygiene before enabling predictive features.** Particularly disqualification and closed-lost reasons.

**Present scores as signals, not decisions.** Automation that acts irreversibly on a probability is a design error.

**Ask about bias during design**, not during a later review.

**Account for the automation load** of prediction fields updating on high-volume objects.

**Prediction Builder is the underrated one.** It solves real problems on custom objects without code and without a data science team.
