# Making It Stick: Habits & Behaviour Change

*Health & Fitness · 2026-10-30*

Almost everyone already knows they should eat better, move more, sleep well and quit tobacco. Knowledge isn't the bottleneck; behaviour is. The thirty notes before this one are only useful if they change what happens in the kitchen at 1 pm and on the walk at 6 pm, every day, for years. This note is about the mechanics of making that happen — for yourself and, harder, for parents who didn't ask for a lifestyle overhaul.

---

## 1. Why willpower fails

**Habits** are behaviours the brain runs on autopilot in response to a cue, freeing up attention. Most daily eating, movement and tobacco use is habitual, not deliberate: the same chai with the same biscuits at the same time, the same pouch of gutkha after lunch.

Relying on motivation to override habit fails because:
- **Motivation fluctuates.** It's high after a scare (a friend's heart attack, a bad report) and fades within weeks.
- **Decisions are tiring.** Every choice that needs willpower competes with everything else in a busy day.
- **The environment keeps triggering the old habit.** If the cue and the reward are still there, the old behaviour returns.

The goal is to **make healthy behaviour the automatic default**, so it takes less willpower than the unhealthy one.

## 2. The habit loop

Behaviour researchers describe habits as a loop:
1. **Cue** — a trigger: a time, place, emotion, person or preceding action (after lunch, feeling stressed, seeing the tobacco shop).
2. **Routine** — the behaviour (chewing gutkha, eating sweets, sitting with the phone).
3. **Reward** — what the brain gets (nicotine hit, relief from boredom or stress, pleasure).

To change a habit:
- **Keep the cue and reward, swap the routine** where possible: after lunch (cue), chew saunf and walk 10 minutes (new routine), for a sense of calm and a fresh mouth (reward).
- **Remove or avoid the cue**: no tobacco at home, a different route that avoids the paan shop, no biscuit tin on the table.
- **Make the reward of the new habit visible**: track it, celebrate small wins.

## 3. Principles that work

### Start tiny
Big plans collapse; tiny ones grow. "Two sit-to-stands after brushing teeth" is more likely to stick than "30 minutes of exercise daily." Once tiny is automatic, expand it. BJ Fogg's research on **Tiny Habits** shows small behaviours anchored to existing routines become automatic surprisingly quickly.

### Attach new habits to existing ones (habit stacking)
"After I [existing habit], I will [new habit]."
- After morning tea, I'll check my BP.
- After lunch, I'll walk for 10 minutes.
- After putting milk on to boil in the evening, I'll do my balance exercise.
- After dinner, I'll put the next day's dal to soak.

### Make specific plans (implementation intentions)
Vague goals ("eat healthier") rarely work. Specific plans of the form **"When X happens, I will do Y"** roughly double follow-through in studies. "When I go to the chakki, I'll ask them to add one part chana to four parts wheat."

### Change the environment
Design the home so the healthy choice is the easy one:
- **Food:** fruit and roasted chana visible; biscuits and namkeen not bought; a smaller roti basket on the table; salad cut and served first; dal made in a larger quantity.
- **Movement:** walking shoes by the door; a resistance band hung near the chair; a chair positioned for sit-to-stands.
- **Tobacco:** none in the house, no spittoons, saunf and elaichi in every pocket.
- **Sleep:** phone charging outside the bedroom; dim lights after dinner.
- **Medicines:** a weekly pill organiser next to the tea things.

### Make it social
Behaviour spreads through families and friends. Walking with a neighbour, a family WhatsApp group for daily steps, quitting tobacco together, or a weekly family weigh-in turn individual effort into shared routine ([[15 - Stress, Mental Health & Social Connection]]).

### Track, but simply
What gets measured gets attention. A wall calendar with ticks for walks, a notebook of BP readings, a step counter. **Don't break the chain** — a visible streak is motivating. Keep it simple enough to continue.

### Focus on identity, not just outcomes
"I'm someone who walks every evening" lasts longer than "I'm trying to lose 5 kg." Each repetition is a vote for the kind of person you're becoming.

### Plan for slips
Everyone slips: a wedding, a festival, an illness, a stressful week. The rule is **"never miss twice"** — one missed walk is normal; two in a row starts a new habit. A slip with tobacco is a reason to restart immediately, not to give up ([[16 - Tobacco, Alcohol & Environmental Exposures]]).

### Expect it to take time
A frequently cited study (Lally et al., 2010) found that new habits took anywhere from about 18 to over 250 days to become automatic, with a median around 66 days. Plan for months, not a weekly challenge.

## 4. Helping parents change: what works and what backfires

Changing your own habits is hard; changing someone else's is harder, especially parents who've eaten, worked and lived a certain way for 50–60 years and see you as their child, not their doctor.

### What backfires
- **Lecturing, scaring and nagging.** These trigger defensiveness ("we've lived this long, haven't we?") and quiet non-compliance.
- **Changing everything at once.** A complete diet overhaul in week one usually ends by week three.
- **Criticising food or cooking.** Food is identity, love and tradition. "Your food is unhealthy" lands as "you've been doing it wrong for 30 years."
- **Taking control** without their agreement. Adults resist being managed.
- **Shame** about weight, tobacco or results.

### What works
1. **Start from their goals, not yours.** Ask what they want: to keep working on the farm, to see grandchildren grow up, to stop knee pain, to avoid hospital. Link every change to that.
2. **Ask, then listen.** "What do you think is making the knee worse?" "What would make walking easier?" This approach, close to what clinicians call **motivational interviewing**, helps people find their own reasons to change.
3. **Use the doctor's authority.** Many Indian parents listen to a doctor more readily than to their children. Go to appointments together, ask the doctor to explain the reports, and let recommendations come from them.
4. **Pick one or two changes at a time**, the ones with the biggest effect and the least resistance. For many families: tobacco cessation and a daily BP check first, then protein and fewer rotis, then strength exercises.
5. **Make it easy and concrete.** Buy the BP monitor and show how to use it; arrange the mixed atta with the chakki; buy the resistance band and do the first session together; put the pill organiser in place.
6. **Make it family-wide.** The whole household eats the same rebalanced thali, quits tobacco together or walks together. No one person is singled out.
7. **Respect tradition and adapt it.** Keep favourite dishes and festivals; adjust portions and proportions. Traditional foods (millets, dal, curd, sabzi) are already the solution.
8. **Celebrate progress.** Point out the falling BP readings, the easier stairs, the lower sugar. Visible results motivate continuation.
9. **Be patient and consistent.** Regular check-ins (a short call every day or two) about how things are going, not just about numbers.
10. **Accept their autonomy.** Adults have the right to make their own choices. Your role is to inform, support and make healthy choices easier — not to control.

## 5. Breaking specific Indian habits

| Habit | Cue | Swap | Environment change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gutkha/khaini after meals | Finishing lunch | Saunf, elaichi or clove; a 10-minute walk; NRT gum if quitting | No pouches at home; avoid the paan shop route |
| Sugary chai 3–4 times a day | Morning, guests, afternoon | Reduce sugar by half a spoon every week; fewer cups | Smaller cups; sugar jar not on the table |
| 4–5 rotis per meal | Serving at the table | Start with salad and dal; 2–3 rotis | Smaller roti basket; second helping of sabzi, not roti |
| Evening namkeen and biscuits | Tea time | Roasted chana, peanuts, fruit, sprouts | Don't buy biscuits and namkeen in bulk |
| Sitting after dinner | TV after dinner | 10–15 minute walk first | Shoes by the door; walk with family |
| Late nights with phone | Lying in bed | Fixed bedtime; reading or prayer | Phone charging outside the bedroom |
| Skipping medicines | Forgetting | Pill organiser by the chai things | Phone alarm; family check |
| Painkillers for every ache | Knee pain | Strength exercises; heat; gel | Exercise band near the chair; physiotherapy plan |

## 6. When habits aren't the whole problem

Sometimes the barrier isn't habit but circumstance:
- **Money:** focus on free and cheap changes first ([[30 - Health Insurance & Money for Health]]).
- **Time:** farm and family work leave little spare time; stack habits into existing routines.
- **Pain:** knee or back pain makes exercise hard; treat it first ([[21 - Joints & Bones - Osteoarthritis, Osteoporosis & Falls]]).
- **Low mood or depression:** saps motivation for everything; treat it as a medical problem ([[15 - Stress, Mental Health & Social Connection]]).
- **Addiction:** tobacco and alcohol dependence are medical conditions; use medicines and counselling, not just willpower.

---

## The layer, in one line

Health changes stick when they're tiny, attached to existing routines, specific, supported by a home environment that makes the healthy choice easy, shared by the family and tracked simply; with parents, start from their goals, use the doctor's authority, change one or two things at a time, respect tradition and autonomy, and be patient for months, not weeks.
