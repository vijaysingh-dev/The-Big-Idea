# Service Cloud Scenario Questions

## Scenario 1

> Cases arrive by email, phone, web form, and now WhatsApp. Agents miss some, and response times are inconsistent.

**Questions first.** Volume per channel? Are the SLAs different by channel or by customer tier? Is there a single team or several? What does "miss" mean — unassigned, or assigned and untouched?

**The design.**

Email-to-Case for email, and I would use **On-Demand rather than the legacy agent** unless there is a specific reason — no server to maintain, and the routing is the same.

Web-to-Case for the form, or a screen flow if the intake needs validation, because Web-to-Case does not run validation rules or assignment rules the way an authenticated path does.

Messaging for WhatsApp, which is the licensed digital engagement channel.

Then **Omni-Channel for routing across all of them**, which is the piece that makes multi-channel work. Assignment rules push to a queue; Omni-Channel pulls to an available agent with capacity, which is what stops cases sitting in a queue nobody watches.

Entitlements and milestones for the SLA — a first-response milestone and a resolution milestone, with escalation actions.

**The tradeoff:** Omni-Channel needs capacity configured realistically. Set it too high and agents are overloaded; too low and cases queue while people are idle. That is a tuning exercise with real data, not a configuration decision.

## Scenario 2

> Agents spend the first two minutes of every call finding out who they are talking to and what happened before.

**Questions.** What do they actually need to know? Is the information in Salesforce, or in another system? How many tabs are they opening?

**The design.**

The Service Console, if they are not on it — the tabbed workspace is the whole point, and agents on a standard record page are working with the wrong tool.

Then the console layout is the design work. A compact layout showing the identity fields in the highlights panel. Related lists for recent cases and open claims. The Case Contact's account and entitlement visible without navigating.

**Where I would use a component rather than configuration:** a summary panel that composes several sources into one view — recent cases, active service contracts, machine coverage — as one Apex call rather than four wires. In a console, where every millisecond multiplies across a day, that composition matters.

Macros for the repetitive parts of case handling — a status update, a templated email, a field set.

**And the honest point:** if the information lives in an ERP, no amount of Salesforce configuration fixes it. That is an integration question, and the answer might be an external object rather than a sync.

## Scenario 3

> The same twenty questions account for half the case volume.

**Questions.** Are they genuinely the same, or superficially similar? Is there existing documentation? Who would maintain the content?

**The design.**

Knowledge, with articles for the top questions and a data category structure that matches how customers think rather than how the org is structured.

Article recommendations on the case, so agents see relevant articles without searching.

Then deflection, which is where the volume actually reduces — a public help centre on Experience Cloud, with the articles marked visible in the public knowledge base, and search that works.

**The maintenance question is the one that decides whether this succeeds.** Articles go stale, and an org with 400 outdated articles is worse than one with 20 current ones. I would want an owner per data category and a review cycle.

**Where an agent fits:** an Agentforce agent answering from the knowledge base handles the straightforward cases and escalates the rest. The design considerations are scoping topics narrowly, grounding on published articles only, and an escalation path to a human — an agent that cannot hand off will eventually trap someone.

## Scenario 4

> Some cases need a specialist, and agents do not know who. Cases bounce between teams.

**Questions.** How is the specialism determined — product, region, complexity? Can it be known at intake, or only after investigation? How often does a case need more than one specialist?

**Two different problems.**

**If it is knowable at intake:** assignment rules or Omni-Channel skills-based routing. Skills are the better mechanism — an agent has skills, a case requires skills, and routing matches them. It handles "needs Hindi and hydraulic press expertise" in a way that queue-based routing does not.

**If it emerges during investigation:** that is a transfer, and the design question is whether the case moves or whether the specialist joins. Case Team is the mechanism for joining — the original agent stays accountable and the specialist contributes.

**Swarming** where several people work a case together, with a Slack channel tied to the case and the conversation captured back onto it.

**The thing I would measure:** how often cases are reassigned. A high rate means the intake is not capturing what routing needs, which is a form design problem rather than a routing problem.

## Scenario 5

> Customers complain they have to repeat themselves. The same issue produces three cases.

**Questions.** Are they genuinely the same issue, or related ones? Is the customer contacting through different channels? Is there a case hierarchy in use?

**The design.**

Parent-child cases for genuinely related issues, so the relationship is visible.

But the more common cause is that the same customer contacted twice and nobody linked it. Duplicate case detection at intake — matching on contact plus subject similarity — surfacing "this contact has an open case" to the agent rather than blocking creation.

For email specifically, thread Id matching so a reply attaches to the existing case rather than creating a new one. That is standard Email-to-Case behaviour and it breaks when the customer replies from a different address or strips the thread token, which is worth knowing.

**Where I would look first:** whether the case is closed prematurely. An agent closing a case that was not resolved produces a second case, and that is a metric problem — if agents are measured on cases closed, they will close cases.

## Scenario 6

> Field engineers need to see their assigned work and log what they did, offline, on a phone.

**Questions.** How many engineers? Do they need scheduling and dispatch, or just a task list? What does offline mean — occasional loss of signal, or genuinely no connectivity for hours?

**The design.**

**Field Service, not a custom mobile app.** Work orders, service appointments, the dispatcher console, and a mobile app with genuine offline capability built in.

Building a custom Mobile SDK app to replicate that is a large ongoing commitment — app store releases, device testing, and a codebase tracking platform changes — and it should only be considered when Field Service specifically cannot do it.

Service territories for geographic assignment, skills for capability matching, and the scheduling optimizer if dispatch complexity warrants it.

**The offline point matters.** The Field Service mobile app syncs and works without connectivity, and the conflict resolution when it reconnects is handled. A custom app has to build that, and it is harder than it looks.

**Where a custom component fits:** inside the Field Service mobile app, for a specific capture requirement the standard forms do not cover.

## Scenario 7

> Management wants to know why handle time is increasing. The reports show it is, but not why.

**Questions.** Increasing across all case types or specific ones? Across all agents or some? Since when — is there a change that correlates?

**The diagnosis.**

Segment the data before theorizing. By case type, by agent tenure, by channel, by product. An average hiding two populations is not actionable — new agents at twelve minutes and experienced ones at four is a training answer, not a system one.

**Then look at what the system is contributing.** How many clicks does a common case take? Is the page slow? Are agents navigating away to find information?

**A specific thing I would check:** whether case handling requires switching between Salesforce and another system. That shows up as handle time and is an integration or an external object question.

**And the measurement caveat:** handle time is a poor metric on its own. Increasing handle time with improving first-contact resolution is an improvement. I would want both before drawing a conclusion.

## Scenario 8

> An entitlement process is not escalating cases that breach SLA, and nobody noticed for two months.

**Questions.** Is the milestone completing incorrectly, or not starting? Is the escalation action failing, or not configured? What does the milestone status show on the affected cases?

**The diagnosis.**

Check whether the entitlement process is active and whether the cases have an entitlement — a case with no entitlement has no milestones, which is the most common cause. The entitlement is assigned by the contact, the account, or a service contract, and a gap in that assignment produces cases silently outside the process.

Then whether the milestone completion criteria are being met unintentionally. A milestone completing on first response, where the definition of first response is broad, completes on an auto-response email.

Then whether the escalation action ran. Time-triggered actions on milestones are async, and a failure produces no visible error.

**The broader point:** this ran for two months because nothing was watching. A report of cases past their milestone target, checked weekly, would have surfaced it. **Alerting on absence rather than only on failure** applies to business processes as much as to jobs — a milestone process that stopped escalating produces no error record.

## Scenario 9

> A high-value customer's issue was mishandled and escalated to the executive team. What would you put in place?

**This is asking about process design, not configuration.**

**Immediately:** a way to identify high-value customers at intake — an account tier field, visible in the console, driving both routing and SLA.

**Then:** a defined escalation path with owners and time thresholds, implemented as an entitlement process with escalation actions rather than as a convention people remember.

**Then:** visibility. A report of open cases on top-tier accounts, on a dashboard the service manager sees daily. Cases that are aging, that have been reassigned more than twice, or that have had no activity for 48 hours.

**And the part usually missed:** a post-incident review process. What made this hard to catch, and what would have caught it earlier. That is the same discipline as a production incident review, and service organizations frequently do not have one.

**The thing I would resist:** building a complex automated escalation matrix in response to one incident. One event is not a pattern, and over-engineering the response is how a service org ends up with an escalation process nobody understands.

---

## How to answer these

**Ask what "the problem" actually is.** "Agents miss cases" could be routing, capacity, or visibility, and the design differs.

**Reach for the packaged capability first.** Omni-Channel, Entitlements, Knowledge, Field Service. Building a custom equivalent is a signal that the platform is not well understood.

**Consider the agent's experience.** Service Cloud questions are frequently productivity questions, and the answer is console design and reducing clicks rather than new automation.

**Name the measurement.** "How would we know this worked" separates a designed solution from a plausible one.

**Question the metric.** Handle time, case volume, and closure rate all drive behaviour, and some of it is behaviour nobody wants.
