# Incidents and Postmortems

#backend #reliability #operations

> **In one line:** During an incident you mitigate first and diagnose second; afterwards you look for the conditions that made the failure possible, not the person who typed the command.

---

## The Idea

```
Detect → Respond → Mitigate → Resolve → Learn
```

⚠️ **The most common mistake during an incident is trying to understand it before stopping it.** Rolling back, shedding load or failing over restores the user experience while you still have no idea what happened — **and you can diagnose a healthy system at leisure.**

💡 **Mitigation and diagnosis are separate activities with different time pressures. Conflating them extends every outage.**

---

## During an Incident

### Roles

| Role | Does |
|---|---|
| **Incident commander** | Coordinates. **Does not debug.** Decides, delegates, communicates |
| Operations lead | Makes the changes |
| Communications lead | Updates the status page and stakeholders |
| Scribe | Timestamps everything |

⚠️ **The commander must not be the person debugging.** The moment they open a terminal, nobody is coordinating — **and coordination is what turns three people working separately into three people working together.**

💡 **For a small team, one person can hold several roles.** What matters is that someone has explicitly decided who is coordinating, out loud.

### The mitigation ladder

```
1. Did we just deploy?            → roll back
2. Did config just change?         → revert it
3. Is one dependency the cause?    → circuit break, degrade
4. Is it load?                     → shed, rate limit, scale
5. Is one tenant the cause?        → throttle them
6. None of the above               → now diagnose
```

💡 **The first two resolve the majority of incidents**, and they require no understanding at all. **"When did it start" and "what changed then" is the fastest path to mitigation.**

⚠️ **Roll back first, understand later.** A rollback that turns out to be unnecessary costs a deploy; a thirty-minute investigation before rolling back costs thirty minutes of outage.

### The timeline is the artefact

```
14:32  Alert: error rate > 5% on document service
14:33  Acknowledged. IC: Vijay
14:35  Deploy at 14:28 identified as the likely cause
14:36  Rollback started
14:41  Rollback complete, error rate falling
14:47  Error rate normal. Incident mitigated
15:20  Root cause: new query missing an index on org_id
```

💡 **Write timestamps as you go.** Reconstructing a timeline afterwards is unreliable, and **the gaps in it are where your process needs work** — the 14:33→14:35 gap is detection-to-diagnosis, the 14:36→14:41 is your rollback time.

### Communication

```
Every 30 minutes, even with nothing new:
  "Still investigating. Document uploads are failing;
   reads are unaffected. Next update at 15:15."
```

⚠️ **Silence is interpreted as "nobody is working on it".** A regular update with no new information is better than an irregular one with detail.

**Say what works, not only what is broken.** "Reads are unaffected" lets customers plan.

## Severity

| Level | Meaning | Response |
|---|---|---|
| **SEV1** | Total outage, or data loss | Page everyone. All hands |
| **SEV2** | Major degradation, many users | Page on-call. Escalate if not mitigated in 30m |
| **SEV3** | Minor degradation, few users | On-call handles in hours |
| SEV4 | Cosmetic or internal only | Next business day |

💡 **Define these in advance and write them down.** During an incident, "is this a SEV1?" is a debate you do not have time for.

⚠️ **Err upward.** Downgrading a SEV1 that turned out to be minor costs an unnecessary page; **upgrading a SEV3 forty minutes late costs forty minutes.**

## Postmortems

### Blameless means something specific

⚠️ **"Blameless" does not mean "we do not discuss what happened".** It means the analysis targets the conditions rather than the person.

```
❌  "Vijay ran the migration without lock_timeout"
✅  "The migration tooling does not set lock_timeout by default, and there is
     no CI check that would have caught it. A reviewer would have had to know
     this from memory."
```

💡 **The test: does the action item prevent the next person from making the same mistake?** "Be more careful" does not; **a linter does** ([[06.11 - Zero Downtime Migrations]]).

**People act reasonably given what they knew at the time.** If the action seems obviously wrong afterwards, **the interesting question is what made it seem reasonable then.**

### The structure

```markdown
## Summary
One paragraph a stakeholder can read.

## Impact
Duration, users affected, requests failed, revenue, data loss.
Be specific. "Some users" is not impact.

## Timeline
Timestamped, from first symptom to resolution.

## Root cause
The chain of conditions, not one line.

## What went well
Genuinely — detection speed, a defence that held, a runbook that worked.

## What went badly
Detection gaps, missing dashboards, wrong runbooks, slow decisions.

## Where we got lucky
⚠️ The most valuable section.

## Action items
Owner, deadline, and a tracking link for each.
```

💡 **"Where we got lucky" is the section most templates omit and the one that finds the next incident.** "The failure happened at 2pm; at 2am nobody would have noticed for hours." "The replica happened to be healthy." **Luck is a dependency you did not know you had.**

### Root cause is a chain

⚠️ **There is rarely one root cause.** Asking "why" five times produces a chain, and **each link is an intervention point.**

```
The document service returned 500s
→ because the database connection pool was exhausted
→ because a new query took 8 seconds
→ because it had no index on org_id
→ because the migration adding the index was in a separate PR that was not merged
→ because there is no check that a query's index exists before the query ships
```

💡 **The last link is where the durable fix lives.** Adding the index fixes this incident; **a check that a query's indexes exist fixes the class.**

**Pick interventions from as far down the chain as is practical.**

## Action Items

| Property | Why |
|---|---|
| **A named owner** | Not a team. A person |
| **A deadline** | Or it is a wish |
| **Tracked where work is tracked** | Not in the postmortem document |
| **Prioritised against other work** | Or it never happens |
| **Specific** | "Improve monitoring" is not an action item |

⚠️ **The most common postmortem failure is action items that are never completed.** A team that writes excellent postmortems and completes 20% of the actions has a documentation practice, not a learning practice.

💡 **Track completion rate as a metric.** Below 70%, you are generating paperwork.

**Cap the number.** Five actions that get done beat fifteen that do not — **and forcing the choice makes you prioritise the ones that matter.**

### The categories worth having

```
Prevent    stop this cause entirely
Detect     find it faster next time
Mitigate   reduce the impact when it recurs
Respond    improve the process
```

💡 **A postmortem with only "prevent" items is incomplete.** You will not prevent everything, **and "we would have detected this in 2 minutes instead of 40" is often worth more than a prevention that only covers one cause.**

## In Production

### The metrics that matter

```python
incidents_total.labels(severity=...).inc()
time_to_detect_seconds.observe(...)
time_to_mitigate_seconds.observe(...)
time_to_resolve_seconds.observe(...)
action_item_completion_ratio.set(...)
```

| Metric | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **Time to detect** | Are your alerts good? |
| **Time to mitigate** | Are your runbooks and tooling good? |
| Time to resolve | Is your architecture debuggable? |
| Incident rate by cause | Where to invest |
| **Action item completion** | Whether you are learning |

💡 **Time to detect is the one to attack first.** An incident detected in 2 minutes and mitigated in 5 is a 7-minute outage; **the same incident detected in 40 minutes is a 45-minute outage, and the mitigation was not the problem.**

⚠️ **A large gap between detection and mitigation means your runbooks are wrong or missing.** A large gap between mitigation and resolution is fine — that is diagnosis, and it is not costing users anything.

### Detection: alert on symptoms

```
❌  "CPU is above 80%"                    — a cause, and often harmless
✅  "Checkout success rate below 99%"     — a symptom users feel
```

💡 **Alert on what users experience, not on what your infrastructure is doing** ([[12.2 - SLI SLO and Error Budgets]]). Cause-based alerts fire constantly during normal operation and get ignored.

⚠️ **The exception is leading indicators with a known consequence** — `age(datfrozenxid)` above 1 billion ([[06.7 - MVCC Vacuum and Bloat]]), certificate expiry, disk filling. **Those are causes worth alerting on because the consequence is severe and the lead time is long.**

### The on-call runbook

```markdown
## Alert: document_service_error_rate_high

**What it means:** more than 1% of document requests are failing.

**First checks (in order):**
1. Recent deploys — `kubectl rollout history deploy/documents`
2. Dependency health — [dashboard link]
3. Database pool — [dashboard link]
4. Error breakdown by type — [query link]

**Common causes:**
- A deploy in the last 30 minutes → roll back
- Search circuit open → expected, degraded, not a SEV2
- Database pool exhausted → check for long transactions

**Mitigation:** rollback, or enable read-only mode ([runbook link])
**Escalate to:** #backend-oncall if not mitigated in 20 minutes
```

⚠️ **A runbook that has never been used during an incident is wrong.** Links rot, dashboards get renamed, tools change. **Game days are how you find that** ([[11.9 - Chaos and Failure Injection]]).

### Reading other people's postmortems

💡 **The highest-leverage reliability reading available.** Every published postmortem is a failure mode you can check your own system against, for free.

```
For each: does this failure mode exist in our system?
          would we detect it?
          would we mitigate it faster?
```

⚠️ **The pattern across almost all of them: the trigger is small and the severity comes from amplification** ([[11.1 - How Systems Fail]]). **Reading three with that lens changes how you evaluate your own systems.**

### Conduit's incident process

| Aspect | |
|---|---|
| Severity levels | SEV1–4, defined in the runbook |
| On-call | One person, weekly rotation |
| Detection | Symptom-based alerts on the SLOs |
| Communication | Status page update within 15 minutes for SEV1/2 |
| Postmortem | Required for SEV1/2, optional for SEV3 |
| Review | Within 5 working days, with the team |
| Action items | Max 5, owned, deadlined, tracked in the issue tracker |

💡 **Requiring a postmortem for SEV3 as well is tempting and usually wrong** — it dilutes the practice and produces documents nobody reads. **Optional, and written when the incident was genuinely surprising.**

## Codebase

**Repo:** Published postmortems; `google/sre` documentation

**Question to answer:** Across three detailed public postmortems, what was the trigger, what amplified it, how long was detection, and what did the action items target? Then: how does Google structure incident response and what does "blameless" mean operationally?

**Read, in order:**

1. **Google SRE Book — "Managing Incidents" and "Postmortem Culture"** — **the reference for both.** The role separation and the blameless definition come from here
2. **Cloudflare's postmortems** — consistently detailed and technical. **Look for the ones involving a configuration change with an unexpected blast radius**
3. **The AWS S3 outage (2017)** — a command with a typo removed too much capacity, **and recovery was slow because subsystems had not been fully restarted in years.** Note where the severity actually came from
4. **GitLab's database incident (2017)** — the trigger was human, **and the severity came from backups that had never been tested.** Written up with unusual honesty
5. **Any postmortem from a company in your domain** — read it asking "would this happen to us"

💡 The GitLab writeup is the one to read closely. **It is transparent about the human factors, and every action item targets a condition rather than the person** — which makes it the clearest worked example of a blameless postmortem in public.

**Size:** 1 session.

## Exercise

**Drill 11.10 — Run an incident, then learn from it**

```
drills/11-incidents/
├── process/{severity.md, roles.md, runbooks/}
├── detection/{symptom_alerts.yaml, cause_alerts.yaml, COMPARISON.md}
├── simulation/{scenarios/, run.py, timeline.py}
├── postmortems/{template.md, completed/}
├── external/{analysis.md}
└── README.md
```

**Part 1 — Define the process.** Severity levels with examples, roles, communication cadence, and the escalation path. **One page, and it must be readable during an incident.**

**Part 2 — Symptom versus cause alerts.** Write both for the same underlying problem: `cpu > 80%` and `checkout_success_rate < 99%`.

Run a realistic workload for a day. **Count how often each fires, and how many firings were actionable.**

**The cause-based alert should fire far more often and be actionable far less.** That ratio is why symptom-based alerting exists.

**Part 3 — Write three runbooks.** For your three most likely alerts, with first checks, common causes, mitigations and escalation.

**Then have someone who did not write them follow each during a simulated incident.** Record every point where they were stuck.

**Part 4 — Run three incidents.** Using the chaos harness from [[11.9 - Chaos and Failure Injection]], inject a failure without telling the responder which.

For each measure: **time to detect, time to correct diagnosis, time to mitigate.** Keep a timestamped timeline as it happens.

**Do all three with different responders if you can.**

**Part 5 — Write the postmortems.** One per simulated incident, using the full template **including "where we got lucky".**

⚠️ **Force yourself to write the luck section.** It will be the hardest and it will produce the most useful findings.

Then write the root cause as a chain of at least four links, **and pick an action item from as far down as practical.**

**Part 6 — Action items that get done.** Cap at five per postmortem, each with an owner and deadline, tracked outside the document.

**Actually complete them.** Then re-run the same incident and **measure the improvement in time to detect and time to mitigate.**

**That before-and-after is the evidence the process works.**

**Part 7 — Analyse three external postmortems.** For each: the trigger, the amplification mechanism, time to detect, and what the action items targeted.

**Then, for each, answer: does this failure mode exist in our system? Would we detect it? Would we mitigate it faster?**

**Turn every "no" into an experiment in your chaos catalogue.**

**Done when:**
- Your severity definitions and roles fit on one readable page
- Symptom alerts demonstrably fire less and are more actionable than cause alerts
- Someone who did not write your runbooks can follow them
- Three simulated incidents have timestamped timelines with all three durations measured
- Every postmortem has a "where we got lucky" section with real content
- Re-running an incident after completing action items is measurably faster
- Three external postmortems produced new experiments in your catalogue
