# Health Insurance & Money for Health

*Health & Fitness · 2026-10-30*

In India, a large share of health spending still comes straight out of people's pockets, and a single hospitalisation — a bypass, a cancer course, dialysis, an ICU stay after dengue — can wipe out years of savings or push a family into debt. Money is part of health. A good plan pairs prevention with protection: cheap habits and tests to avoid illness, and insurance and savings so that when illness comes, treatment isn't delayed for lack of money.

> This note gives general information, not financial or insurance advice. Rules and products change; read policy documents and check current rules before buying.

---

## 1. Why this matters

- **Out-of-pocket spending** (paid directly by patients rather than through insurance or government) has historically made up a large share of total health spending in India, and is a well-documented cause of families falling into poverty.
- **Delay is deadly.** Families without cover often postpone tests and treatment until disease is advanced ([[24 - Silent Killers - Diseases That Hide Until Late]]).
- **Prevention is cheap; treatment is expensive.** A year of BP tablets and a home BP monitor costs far less than one day in an ICU after a stroke.

## 2. Government schemes worth knowing

### Ayushman Bharat PM-JAY
**Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana** provides health cover of up to **₹5 lakh per family per year** for hospitalisation at empanelled public and private hospitals, cashless, for eligible poorer and vulnerable families (based on socio-economic criteria). Since late 2024, cover has been extended to **all senior citizens aged 70 and above, regardless of income** (Ayushman Vay Vandana card). Check eligibility on the official PM-JAY website or at a Common Service Centre, and get the **Ayushman card** made in advance.

### State schemes
Many states run their own health insurance schemes (some merged with PM-JAY) with different coverage and eligibility. Check your state's scheme.

### Other programmes
- **CGHS** (Central Government Health Scheme) and **ESIC** (Employees' State Insurance) for eligible government and organised-sector workers and their families.
- **Free services** at government facilities: NP-NCD screening, BP and diabetes medicines, TB treatment, dialysis under the Pradhan Mantri National Dialysis Programme, vaccines, maternal care ([[27 - The Testing Calendar]], [[28 - Medicines Without Myths]]).
- **Jan Aushadhi** generic medicine stores.

## 3. Private health insurance: the basics

### Key terms
- **Sum insured:** the maximum the policy pays in a year.
- **Premium:** what you pay yearly for the policy.
- **Individual vs family floater:** an individual policy covers one person; a **family floater** shares one sum insured across family members. Floaters are cheaper, but one big claim can use up the cover for everyone. Parents are often better on a **separate policy**, since their age raises the floater premium for the whole family.
- **Pre-existing disease (PED):** any condition diagnosed or treated before buying the policy — diabetes, high BP, thyroid disease, heart disease. Must be **declared honestly**.
- **PED waiting period:** time before claims related to a pre-existing disease are covered. Under IRDAI regulations, the maximum is now **36 months (3 years)** of continuous cover; some policies offer shorter waits or reduce them for an extra premium.
- **Initial waiting period:** usually 30 days for most illnesses (accidents are covered from day one).
- **Specific-disease waiting periods:** for certain listed conditions or procedures (cataract, hernia, joint replacement and others), typically 1–3 years.
- **Moratorium period:** after **5 years of continuous coverage**, an insurer generally can't reject a claim for non-disclosure of a pre-existing condition, except in cases of fraud.
- **Co-payment (co-pay):** a percentage of each claim you pay yourself (often 10–30% in senior citizen policies).
- **Room rent limit:** a cap on the room category or daily room charge. Choosing a more expensive room than the cap can reduce payment on the **whole bill** proportionally, not just the room. Policies with no room rent cap are preferable.
- **Sub-limits:** caps on specific treatments (for example, cataract or certain surgeries).
- **Cashless vs reimbursement:** cashless means the insurer pays an empanelled (network) hospital directly; reimbursement means you pay first and claim later.
- **No-claim bonus:** an increase in sum insured for each claim-free year.
- **Restoration/recharge:** the sum insured is refilled if used up within a year.
- **Pre- and post-hospitalisation cover:** tests and medicines for a set number of days before and after admission.
- **Day-care procedures:** treatments not needing a 24-hour stay (cataract, dialysis, chemotherapy).
- **OPD cover:** most basic policies don't cover outpatient visits, tests or medicines; some offer it as an add-on.
- **Super top-up:** an inexpensive extra policy that pays once total yearly bills cross a set amount (the **deductible**). Combining a base policy with a super top-up is a cost-effective way to get high cover.

### Recent rule changes (check current status)
IRDAI reforms from 2024 onwards removed the upper age limit for buying new health insurance, cut the maximum PED waiting period from 4 to 3 years, reduced the moratorium period from 8 to 5 years, and placed limits on premium increases for senior citizens. Individual health insurance premiums were exempted from GST from September 2025. Confirm current rules before buying.

### Choosing a policy: a checklist
1. **Adequate sum insured** for your city's hospital costs; many advisers suggest at least ₹5–10 lakh base cover per person in larger cities, topped up with a super top-up.
2. **No room rent cap**, or a generous one.
3. **Low or no co-pay**, where affordable.
4. **Short PED waiting period**, or the option to reduce it.
5. **Few sub-limits.**
6. **A wide network** including hospitals near home.
7. **Good claim settlement record** and transparent service (IRDAI publishes insurers' claim data).
8. **Lifelong renewability** (now standard).
9. **Read the policy wording and exclusions list**, not just the brochure.

### Buying for parents
- **Buy early.** Premiums rise with age, and each new diagnosis adds to waiting periods and exclusions.
- **Declare everything** — diabetes, BP, thyroid, past surgeries, tobacco use. Non-disclosure is a common reason for rejected claims.
- **Expect a medical check** for older applicants; insurers may load premiums or add co-pays.
- **Senior citizen–specific policies** exist, often with co-pays but sometimes shorter waiting periods for some conditions.
- **Check whether your employer's group policy allows adding parents**; group cover often covers pre-existing diseases from day one, but ends when you leave the job, so it shouldn't be the only protection.
- **Plan cash for the waiting period**: for the first 1–3 years, treatment of declared pre-existing conditions may not be covered, so keep an emergency fund.

### Tax benefit
Under **Section 80D** of the Income Tax Act (old tax regime), premiums paid for health insurance qualify for deductions: up to ₹25,000 a year for self, spouse and children (₹50,000 if the policyholder is a senior citizen), and an additional ₹25,000 for parents (₹50,000 if they're senior citizens, 60+). Up to ₹5,000 for preventive health check-ups falls within these limits. The new tax regime doesn't allow these deductions; check which regime applies.

### Making a claim smoothly
- **Keep the policy number, insurer helpline and TPA** (third-party administrator) details in the family health card.
- **Planned admission:** request cashless pre-authorisation in advance.
- **Emergency admission:** inform the insurer within the time stated in the policy (often 24 hours).
- **Keep all documents:** prescriptions, reports, bills, discharge summary.

## 4. Building a family health budget

A simple framework for a monthly health budget, adjusting the amounts to income:

| Category | What it covers | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Protection** | Health insurance premiums (spread monthly) | Highest priority; buy before illness |
| **Emergency fund** | Savings for waiting periods, co-pays, uncovered costs | A separate, easily accessible fund for health; many planners suggest several months' expenses |
| **Prevention** | Yearly core lab panel, eye and dental checks, vaccines | Use free government screening where available |
| **Home monitoring** | BP monitor, glucometer and strips, scale, tape, pulse oximeter | One-time costs plus strips |
| **Medicines** | Ongoing prescribed medicines | Use generics and Jan Aushadhi to cut costs |
| **Food upgrades** | More dal, dairy, vegetables, fruit, nuts, seeds; protein supplement if needed | Mostly rebalancing ([[11 - The Indian Plate - Rebuilding the Thali]]) |
| **Movement** | Resistance bands, footwear; physiotherapy sessions if needed | Mostly free |
| **Avoid** | Expensive "premium" test packages, untested supplements, "immunity boosters," herbal cures | Little benefit, sometimes harm |

**At a tight budget:** Ayushman Bharat if eligible, free government screening and medicines, a BP monitor, and food rebalancing cover most of the benefit.

**At a moderate budget:** add private insurance (base plus super top-up), a yearly core panel at an accredited lab, vaccines and dental care.

**At a comfortable budget:** add higher sum insured, OPD cover if useful, periodic dietitian or physiotherapist sessions, and a slightly broader testing panel as advised by a doctor.

## 5. Money mistakes to avoid

- **Waiting until a diagnosis** to buy insurance.
- **Relying only on employer insurance.**
- **Hiding pre-existing conditions** on the proposal form.
- **Choosing the cheapest policy** without checking room rent caps, co-pays and sub-limits.
- **Spending on unproven supplements and alternative "cures"** while skipping proven tests and medicines.
- **Stopping prescribed medicines to save money**, rather than asking for cheaper generics.
- **Borrowing from informal moneylenders** for emergencies when scheme cover or hospital financial-aid desks could help; ask hospitals about social-work and financial assistance, especially for cancer and heart surgery.

---

## The layer, in one line

Health and money are tied together in India: use government schemes like Ayushman Bharat and free screening where eligible, buy private health insurance early with honest disclosure while understanding waiting periods, room caps and co-pays, keep an emergency fund for the gaps, and spend the rest on proven prevention — not on premium test packages and unproven products.
