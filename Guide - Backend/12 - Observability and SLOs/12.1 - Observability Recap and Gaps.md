# Observability Recap and Gaps

#backend #observability #architecture

> **In one line:** You already have a full observability guide — this note is the part it does not cover: what to instrument in a backend specifically, what the three pillars each cannot tell you, and where every system has the same blind spots.

---

## What You Already Have

| Guide | Covers |
|---|---|
| **Observability — Complete Guide** | Metrics, logs, traces, OpenTelemetry, Prometheus, collectors, sampling, storage |

**This note does not repeat it.** It covers three things a general guide cannot:

1. **The backend-specific instrumentation** — what to measure in each layer you have built
2. **What each pillar cannot answer**, and where they must be combined
3. **The gaps every system has**, and how to find yours

---

## The Three Pillars, and Their Blind Spots

| Pillar | Answers | **Cannot answer** |
|---|---|---|
| **Metrics** | "How many, how fast, how often" | **"Why this particular request"** |
| **Logs** | "What happened in this one execution" | **"Is this normal"** |
| **Traces** | "Where did the time go across services" | **"How often does this happen"** |

💡 **Every real investigation crosses at least two.** A latency alert (metric) leads to a slow trace (trace) which points at a log line with the failing query (log). **A system with only one pillar produces investigations that stall.**

### The specific gaps

⚠️ **Metrics cannot tell you about the request that mattered.** A p99 of 800ms tells you 1% of requests are slow; it does not tell you *which* ones, or what they had in common. **Exemplars close this gap** — a metric sample that carries a trace ID.

```python
histogram.observe(elapsed, exemplar={"trace_id": current_trace_id()})
```

💡 **Exemplars are the single most underused observability feature.** They turn "p99 is 800ms" into "click here to see a request that took 800ms", **and they are a few lines to enable.**

⚠️ **Logs cannot tell you whether something is unusual** without aggregation, at which point you have rebuilt metrics badly and expensively.

⚠️ **Traces are sampled**, so the trace you want may not exist. **Tail-based sampling** — decide after the request completes, keeping all errors and slow requests — fixes this and costs more.

## What to Instrument, Per Layer

Working through what you have built:

### The request path

```python
http_requests_total.labels(route=..., method=..., status=...).inc()
http_request_duration_seconds.labels(route=...).observe(elapsed)
http_requests_in_flight.labels(route=...).set(n)
```

⚠️ **Label by *route*, not path.** `/documents/doc_42` as a label value produces unbounded cardinality and will take down your metrics backend ([[10.6 - API Gateway and BFF]]).

💡 **`in_flight` is the one people omit and it is the best overload signal** — it rises before latency does, because requests queue internally before they slow down ([[11.8 - Capacity Planning and Load Testing]]).

### The event loop

```python
event_loop_lag_seconds.observe(lag)
```

💡 **Loop lag is the single most important metric in an async Python service** ([[01.12 - Profiling and Debugging Async Services]]). Rising lag means something is blocking the loop, and **it explains "everything got slow at once" better than any other signal.**

### Connection pools — all three of them

```python
pool_acquire_wait_seconds.labels(pool="postgres").observe(elapsed)
pool_connections_in_use.labels(pool=...).set(n)
```

⚠️ **There are three queues between your code and the database** — your application pool, PgBouncer, and Postgres itself ([[06.9 - Connection Pooling and PgBouncer]]). **Instrument all three, or you will investigate the wrong one.**

### The database

```python
db_query_duration_seconds.labels(operation=...).observe(elapsed)
db_queries_per_request.observe(count)          # ← N+1 detection
```

💡 **`db_queries_per_request` is the metric that catches N+1 regressions in production** ([[05.6 - The N+1 Problem and DataLoader]]). **Alert when p99 jumps.**

Plus the Postgres-side signals from [[06.2 - Postgres Architecture]]: cache hit ratio, dead tuples, replication lag, and **`age(datfrozenxid)`.**

### Caches

```python
cache_requests_total.labels(layer=..., prefix=..., result=...).inc()
cache_stale_served_total.inc()
```

**Per prefix, not globally** — a global 80% hit ratio hides one prefix at 5% doing all the damage ([[07.1 - Cache Layers Across One Request]]).

### Background work

```python
queue_depth.labels(queue=...).set(depth)
queue_oldest_message_age_seconds.labels(queue=...).set(age)      # ← the key one
task_duration_seconds.labels(task=...).observe(elapsed)
dead_letter_total.labels(task=...).inc()
```

💡 **Alert on oldest-age, not depth** ([[08.1 - Why Background Work Exists]]).

### Dependencies

```python
dependency_duration_seconds.labels(dep=...).observe(elapsed)
dependency_errors_total.labels(dep=..., kind="timeout"|"connection"|"5xx").inc()
circuit_state.labels(dep=...).set(0|1|2)
concurrency_in_use.labels(dep=...).set(n)
```

⚠️ **`concurrency_in_use` per dependency is the early warning for resource exhaustion** ([[11.1 - How Systems Fail]]).

### Resilience mechanisms

```python
requests_shed_total.labels(priority=...).inc()
degraded_responses_total.labels(reason=...).inc()
retry_budget_consumed_ratio.labels(dep=...).set(r)
lock_fenced_out_total.inc()
```

💡 **These are the metrics that tell you whether your section 11 work is actually functioning.** `degraded_responses_total` by reason names the dependency hurting users right now, **which is more actionable than a raw error rate.**

⚠️ **A system that degrades silently looks healthy** — error rates low, latency fine, users getting a worse product. **Alert on sustained degradation, not only on errors** ([[11.6 - Graceful Degradation]]).

## The Gaps Every System Has

### 1. The queue boundary

⚠️ **Traces end at "published to Kafka" and a new one starts in the consumer.** Two disconnected halves.

```python
carrier = {}
TraceContextTextMapPropagator().inject(carrier)
await publish(topic, payload, headers=carrier)
```

💡 **The most commonly missing propagation, and the most painful** ([[12.3 - Instrumenting Conduit End to End]]). "Why did this document take 40 seconds to index" is unanswerable without it.

### 2. Per-tenant attribution

⚠️ **"The system is slow" and "one customer is slow" look identical in aggregate metrics.**

```python
sql = f"/* org:{org_id} */ SELECT ..."
```

**Query tagging costs nothing and makes per-tenant attribution possible in every tool** ([[06.16 - Multi Tenant Data Models]]).

⚠️ **Do not label metrics by tenant** — unbounded cardinality. **Export the top N by usage, or aggregate by plan tier.**

### 3. Client-perceived reality

⚠️ **Server-side metrics miss everything between your load balancer and the user** — DNS, TLS handshake, network, and requests that never arrived.

💡 **Gray failure is invisible from the server** ([[11.1 - How Systems Fail]]): the service reports healthy, some requests succeed, and a subset fails for a reason no probe checks. **Measure from the client's perspective, via real-user monitoring or synthetic checks.**

### 4. The cost dimension

```python
db_rows_read_total.labels(route=...).inc(rows)
external_api_calls_total.labels(provider=...).inc()
```

💡 **Cost per request per endpoint turns capacity from an engineering question into a business one** ([[11.8 - Capacity Planning and Load Testing]]), and **it usually reveals one expensive endpoint dominating spend while being a small fraction of traffic.**

### 5. What is deployed

```python
build_info.labels(version=..., commit=..., service=...).set(1)
```

⚠️ **Label every metric with the version.** A rolling deploy where only the new pods are broken looks like a 50% error rate rather than a version problem ([[10.10 - Deployment Strategies]]).

## In Production

### Cardinality is the operational constraint

```
Series = product of all label value counts
```

| Label | Values | Safe? |
|---|---|---|
| `route` | ~50 | Yes |
| `status` | ~10 | Yes |
| `method` | ~6 | Yes |
| **`user_id`** | **millions** | **No** |
| **`document_id`** | **millions** | **No** |
| `org_id` | thousands | ⚠️ Depends |

⚠️ **One high-cardinality label can generate millions of series and take down your metrics backend.** It is the most common self-inflicted observability outage.

💡 **High-cardinality dimensions belong in traces and logs, not metrics.** That is precisely the division of labour between the pillars — **and exemplars are the bridge.**

### Structured logging

```python
log.info("document indexed", document_id=doc.id, org_id=org.id,
         chunk_count=n, duration_ms=elapsed * 1000)
```

⚠️ **Log lines that require a regex to query are not structured.** Every field that might be filtered on should be a field.

💡 **Bind context once per request or task**, so every subsequent line carries it:

```python
structlog.contextvars.bind_contextvars(request_id=..., org_id=..., trace_id=...)
```

**The trace ID in every log line is what connects the pillars.** Without it, finding the logs for a slow trace is a manual search by timestamp.

### Sampling

| Strategy | |
|---|---|
| **Head-based** | Decide at the start. Cheap, and you may drop the interesting one |
| **Tail-based** | Decide after completion. Keep all errors and slow requests. **More expensive, much more useful** |

💡 **Tail-based sampling with "keep 100% of errors and anything over p99, plus 1% of the rest" gives you almost all the value at a fraction of the volume.**

⚠️ **Sampling must be consistent across services**, or you get partial traces. **Propagate the sampling decision in the trace context.**

### The cost of observability

⚠️ **Observability data volume frequently exceeds application data volume**, and the bill is often a surprise.

| Lever | |
|---|---|
| Drop high-cardinality labels | The biggest single win |
| Tail-based sampling | Keep the useful traces only |
| Log level discipline | `DEBUG` in production is expensive and rarely read |
| Metric retention tiers | High resolution for days, downsampled for months |
| **Delete unused dashboards and alerts** | They still cost queries |

💡 **Audit which metrics are actually queried.** Most systems export hundreds of series nobody has ever looked at.

### The gap audit

For your own system, answer:

```
□  Can I trace one request through every service and queue as one trace?
□  Can I attribute cost and latency to a specific tenant?
□  Would I detect a gray failure (some requests fail, health checks pass)?
□  Can I tell which deployed version a metric came from?
□  Does a p99 alert let me find an actual slow request?
□  Do I know which endpoint costs the most per request?
□  Would I know if a degradation path activated?
□  Would I know if a scheduled job silently stopped running?
```

⚠️ **The last one is the quietest failure in a backend system** ([[08.8 - Periodic Tasks and Distributed Cron]]) — no error, no log line, no alert.

**Every "no" is a gap worth closing before the next incident finds it for you.**

## Codebase

**Repo:** `open-telemetry/opentelemetry-python`, `prometheus/prometheus`, `grafana/tempo`

**Question to answer:** How does OpenTelemetry propagate trace context across a process boundary, and what does the W3C `traceparent` header contain? Then: how does tail-based sampling work — where is the decision made, and what does it cost?

**Read, in order:**

1. `opentelemetry-python/opentelemetry-api/src/opentelemetry/trace/propagation/tracecontext.py` — **`traceparent` inject and extract.** **The answer to the first question**, and it is a short, well-specified format
2. `opentelemetry-python/opentelemetry-sdk/src/opentelemetry/sdk/trace/sampling.py` — head-based sampling, including `ParentBased` and why the parent's decision must be honoured
3. `grafana/tempo/modules/distributor/` and the tail sampling processor in the OTel Collector — **buffering spans until the trace completes, then deciding.** **The answer to the second question, and where the memory cost comes from**
4. `prometheus/prometheus/tsdb/` — how series are stored, and **why cardinality is the binding constraint**
5. `open-telemetry/opentelemetry-collector-contrib/processor/tailsamplingprocessor/` — the policies: latency, status code, rate limiting, and composite

💡 The tail sampling processor is the read that explains the cost. **It must hold every span of every in-flight trace in memory until the trace is complete**, which means memory proportional to trace rate × trace duration. **That is why it runs in a collector rather than in your application, and why the decision window is bounded.**

**Size:** 1 session.

## Exercise

**Drill 12.1 — Find your blind spots**

```
drills/12-observability/
├── instrument/{http.py, loop.py, pools.py, db.py, cache.py, queue.py, deps.py}
├── gaps/{queue_boundary.py, tenant_attribution.py, gray_failure.py, version_labels.py}
├── cardinality/{explosion.py, audit.py, RESULTS.md}
├── exemplars/{enable.py, metric_to_trace.py}
├── sampling/{head.py, tail.py, COMPARISON.md}
├── cost/{volume.py, levers.py, RESULTS.md}
└── audit.md
```

**Part 1 — Instrument every layer.** Work through the seven layers in this note against Conduit. **Every metric, correctly labelled.**

**Then check cardinality:** compute the series count each metric will produce at your scale.

**Part 2 — Cause a cardinality explosion.** Add `document_id` as a label on a request metric and run realistic traffic.

**Measure series count, Prometheus memory, and query latency over 30 minutes.** Then remove it and confirm recovery.

**This is the most common self-inflicted observability outage, and producing it once is worth the hour.**

Then audit your existing metrics: **which are actually queried by a dashboard or alert?** Delete the rest and measure the volume reduction.

**Part 3 — Close the queue boundary.** Trace a document upload through: HTTP request → database write → outbox → Kafka → consumer → search index.

**Confirm it appears as one trace.**

Then remove the header propagation and **confirm it splits into disconnected halves. Screenshot both.**

**Then measure: with the broken trace, how long does it take to answer "why did this document take 40 seconds to index"?**

**Part 4 — Per-tenant attribution.** Add query tagging and per-tenant cost tracking (rows read, external calls, CPU time).

**Then have one tenant run expensive queries and confirm you can identify them from logs alone**, without a metric labelled by tenant ([[06.16 - Multi Tenant Data Models]]).

**Part 5 — Gray failure.** A service returning 200 for 90% of requests and hanging on 10%, with a health check touching only a trivial endpoint.

**Confirm the health check reports healthy throughout.**

Measure how long until you notice from server-side metrics alone. **Then add client-side success-rate monitoring and measure again.**

**Part 6 — Exemplars.** Enable exemplars on your latency histograms.

**Then go from a p99 alert to an actual slow trace in one click.** Measure the time saved versus searching by timestamp.

**Part 7 — Head versus tail sampling.** Run both at 1% under load with a 2% error rate.

**Measure: what fraction of errors were captured, what fraction of slow requests, storage volume, and collector memory.**

**Head-based should miss most errors; tail-based should capture all of them.** That contrast is the argument.

**Part 8 — The gap audit.** Run the eight-item checklist against Conduit.

**For every "no", implement the fix and verify it** by producing the failure it would miss.

**Done when:**
- A cardinality explosion is reproduced and recovered from
- You deleted metrics nobody queries, with a measured volume reduction
- One document traces cleanly through six hops including a queue
- You can attribute cost to a tenant from logs alone
- A gray failure passed health checks and client-side monitoring caught it
- Exemplars take you from a p99 alert to a slow trace in one click
- Tail sampling captures 100% of errors where head-based missed most
- Every gap-audit item is a "yes", verified by producing the failure
