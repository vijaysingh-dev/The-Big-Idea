# Human Body & Nutrition Basics

*Health & Fitness · 2026-09-12*

Before muscle growth, diets, or supplements make sense, you need the four systems everything else in this topic will build on: energy, structure, regulation, and the gut. This note is the floor everything else stands on — read it once, and diet fads, gym-bro claims, and supplement marketing all start sounding a lot more predictable.

---

## 1. The body runs on energy balance

Every function in your body — thinking, breathing, digesting, lifting a weight — costs energy, measured in **calories** (a unit of energy; in nutrition, technically a kilocalorie, but used interchangeably. 1 food "Calorie" = 1,000 physics calories, which is why nutrition labels use a capital C).

- **Maintenance** is the number of calories your body burns in a day just existing plus whatever activity you do. It is not fixed — it shifts with body weight, muscle mass, activity level, and even sleep and stress. A 90 kg man and a 55 kg woman doing the same job have meaningfully different maintenance numbers before either of them has trained a day in their life.
- Eat above maintenance consistently → the body stores the surplus, mostly as fat, some as muscle if you're also training.
- Eat below maintenance consistently → the body draws on stored energy to make up the gap, mostly fat, but muscle too under the wrong conditions (more on this below).
- This is **energy balance**, and it is the single most reliable lever in health and fitness. Nearly everything else — meal timing, food "quality," supplements — is a smaller effect layered on top of this larger one. This is also *why* wildly different diets (keto, vegan, intermittent fasting, carnivore) can all "work" for weight loss for different people: they're different routes to the same destination, an calorie deficit, not different mechanisms.

Maintenance itself is made of four pieces, often bundled together as **TDEE (Total Daily Energy Expenditure)**:

- **BMR (Basal Metabolic Rate)** — energy burned at total rest just to keep organs running (heart, brain, liver, kidneys). The biggest chunk, usually 60–70% of total daily burn. This is why crash diets rarely work long-term: the body doesn't know the difference between "dieting" and "famine," so a severe, sustained deficit causes it to lower BMR to protect itself — a slowdown sometimes called **adaptive thermogenesis** or, informally, "starvation mode." It's real, but it's an adjustment of maybe 10–15%, not the dramatic "your metabolism breaks forever" story diet culture sometimes tells.
- **Activity** — deliberate exercise: gym sessions, sports, runs.
- **NEAT (Non-Exercise Activity Thermogenesis)** — everything else that moves your body: fidgeting, walking, typing, standing, taking the stairs. This varies far more between people than most realize — studies on overfeeding have found some people unconsciously burn off hundreds of extra calories a day through NEAT alone, which is a big part of why two people can eat similarly and look different.
- **TEF (Thermic Effect of Food)** — energy spent digesting food itself. Protein costs the most to digest (roughly 20–30% of its own calories, versus ~5–10% for carbs and ~0–3% for fat), which is one reason it's often called useful for fat loss: some of what you eat is spent just processing what you ate.

## 2. The body is built and repaired, not just powered

Food isn't only fuel — it's also raw material. This is the part pure "calories in, calories out" thinking misses: two diets at the same calorie total can produce very different bodies depending on what those calories are made of.

- **Protein** breaks down into **amino acids** (protein's building blocks — about 20 of them, 9 of which the body can't make itself and must get from food, called "essential"). The body uses amino acids to build and repair muscle, skin, enzymes, and hormones. Protein is the only macronutrient with a hard structural job that neither carbs nor fat can substitute for.
- **Carbohydrates** break down into **glucose** (blood sugar), the body's preferred fast-access fuel, and are stored short-term as **glycogen** (glucose bundled up in muscle and liver for later use — roughly 400–500g worth in an average adult, which is why a hard training block can visibly deplete "size" that isn't fat, just water bound to glycogen).
- **Fats** break down into fatty acids, used for long-term energy storage, hormone production (including testosterone and estrogen, both built from cholesterol — a reason very-low-fat diets can disrupt hormones), and building cell membranes.

None of these three (called **macronutrients**, because you need them in large amounts, versus **micronutrients** — vitamins and minerals — needed in small amounts, covered in the next note) is optional. The debate in diet culture is about *ratios*, not whether to eliminate one entirely, and extreme elimination diets usually work (when they do) by accidentally fixing energy balance — cutting an entire food category makes overeating harder — not because the excluded macronutrient was toxic.

## 3. Change requires a stimulus and a repair window

The body only changes structure when given a reason to. It's fundamentally lazy: building or maintaining muscle is metabolically expensive, so without a signal that it's needed, the body will let it go. For muscle specifically:

- Resistance training — especially with **progressive overload** (gradually increasing the demand on a muscle over time, via weight, reps, or difficulty) — causes microscopic damage to muscle fibers. This is a genuine, deliberate stressor, which is why soreness (**DOMS**, delayed-onset muscle soreness) is common after training something new or after increasing load, and why soreness itself is a poor indicator of whether a workout "worked" — it fades as the body adapts even when the training stays effective.
- The body repairs this damage through **muscle protein synthesis (MPS)**, using amino acids from dietary protein as raw material.
- The muscle doesn't just repair to its old state — under the right stimulus, it adapts to be slightly more capable next time. This adaptation is **hypertrophy** (increase in muscle fiber size). This is also why "muscle confusion" as a training philosophy is mostly a myth: consistent, progressively harder stress on a specific movement pattern is what drives adaptation, not novelty for its own sake.
- Protein without the training stimulus doesn't build muscle — extra amino acids just get used for energy or stored as fat. Training without enough protein blunts the repair, so the same workout produces less adaptation. Both are required; neither substitutes for the other.

Fuel priority during effort roughly follows a hierarchy: blood glucose first (seconds to minutes), then glycogen (hours), then fat stores (progressively, as glycogen depletes) — protein (muscle tissue) is the last resort, burned only in prolonged starvation or extreme, sustained deficit. This hierarchy is also the biological reason behind why very low-carb diets can leave people feeling flat in the gym initially: the body's preferred fast fuel is scarce, before it adapts to relying more on fat (a process sometimes called **keto-adaptation**, taking one to a few weeks).

## 4. The body is regulated, not just fed

Two systems quietly control how everything above plays out, and both are frequently underrated next to diet:

- **Sleep** — this is when the majority of physical repair and hormonal regulation happens, including the bulk of growth hormone release. Poor sleep raises **cortisol** (a stress hormone) and disrupts hunger-regulating hormones — it lowers **leptin** (which signals fullness) and raises **ghrelin** (which signals hunger) — making fat loss harder and muscle recovery slower, independent of what you eat. A single bad night measurably increases next-day appetite and cravings for high-calorie food; this isn't willpower, it's chemistry working against you.
- **Stress** — chronic (not occasional) stress keeps cortisol elevated, which promotes fat storage, particularly **visceral fat** (around internal organs, more metabolically harmful than fat stored just under the skin), and can interfere with sleep and recovery — a feedback loop where poor sleep raises stress hormones, which worsens sleep further.

Diet and training are the levers people focus on because they're visible and controllable — you can see a plate of food or a workout log. Sleep and stress are just as load-bearing, just less visible, which is why two people can eat and train identically and get different results if one is sleeping five hours a night under constant stress and the other isn't.

## 5. The gut is a live ecosystem, not a pipe

Digestion isn't passive plumbing — it's run by the **gut microbiome**, trillions of bacteria (outnumbering human cells in the body) living in your digestive tract, mostly the large intestine.

- **Probiotics** are live beneficial bacteria (found in fermented foods like yogurt, kimchi, and sauerkraut, or in supplements).
- **Prebiotics** are fiber that these bacteria ferment and feed on — found in foods like garlic, onions, oats, and bananas.
- Probiotics without prebiotics is like adding workers with no food supply for them — introduced bacteria often don't establish themselves long-term without something to eat.
- What matters more than raw bacteria count is **diversity** — a wider range of bacterial species in the gut is linked to better digestion, immune function, and even mood regulation. This last link is not incidental: the gut produces a large share of the body's serotonin (a neurotransmitter tied to mood), and the gut-brain connection runs both ways via the **vagus nerve** — a reason gut health and mental health research increasingly overlap.

---

## The floor, in one line

Energy balance decides whether you gain or lose mass. Training plus protein decides whether that mass is muscle or fat. Sleep and stress decide how efficiently any of this actually works. Gut health decides how well you absorb and process all of it in the first place.

Everything else in this topic — RDAs, vitamins, micronutrients, specific diets, training splits — will sit on top of these four ideas.
