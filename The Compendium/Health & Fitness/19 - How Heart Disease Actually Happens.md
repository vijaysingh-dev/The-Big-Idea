# How Heart Disease Actually Happens

*Health & Fitness · 2026-10-30*

Plenty of Indians who eat little oil, never miss their morning walk and are "not fat" still end up with three blocked arteries in their fifties. That isn't bad luck. Heart disease is built slowly by a handful of specific forces, and "eat less oil" addresses only a small part of one of them.

---

## 1. The heart is a pump that feeds itself last

[[05 - Blood, Circulation & the Heart]] covered how the heart pumps blood around the body. The heart is a muscle that beats around 100,000 times a day, and like any muscle it needs its own blood supply, delivered by three main **coronary arteries** (the arteries that wrap around the heart's surface). They're only a few millimetres wide.

A **heart attack** (**myocardial infarction**, "MI") happens when one of these arteries is suddenly blocked and the muscle beyond it is starved of oxygen. Heart muscle starts dying within minutes, and dead heart muscle doesn't grow back. This is why "time is muscle" is the first rule of emergency cardiology ([[25 - Fast Killers - Emergencies & Warning Signs]]).

The underlying disease is **atherosclerosis**: the slow build-up of fatty, inflamed deposits called **plaques** in the artery wall. When doctors talk about **ASCVD** (atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease), they mean the family of problems this causes: heart attack, angina, stroke and blocked leg arteries.

## 2. How a plaque is built: particles, not "fat in the blood"

The common picture of cholesterol "clogging pipes like grease in a drain" is wrong in an important way. Plaque forms **inside** the artery wall, not on its surface.

Cholesterol and fats don't dissolve in blood, so they travel inside protein-wrapped packages called **lipoproteins**. Think of them as delivery trucks. The ones that matter most:

- **LDL** (low-density lipoprotein) carries cholesterol out to the body's tissues. Called "bad cholesterol."
- **HDL** (high-density lipoprotein) carries excess cholesterol back to the liver. Called "good cholesterol," though raising it with drugs hasn't proven protective, so treat low HDL as a warning sign rather than a target in itself.
- **VLDL** and its leftovers carry triglycerides (the body's main storage fat) and also contribute to plaque.
- **Lp(a)** ("L-P-little-a") is an LDL-like particle with an extra sticky protein attached. More on it below.

Every one of the harmful particles (LDL, VLDL, its remnants and Lp(a)) carries exactly one protein called **ApoB** (apolipoprotein B). That makes the ApoB count a direct count of harmful trucks on the road.

The process, step by step:

1. **Damage to the lining.** The artery's inner lining (the **endothelium**, a single layer of cells) gets irritated by high blood pressure, high sugar, tobacco chemicals and inflammation.
2. **Particles slip into the wall.** ApoB particles cross the damaged lining and get trapped in the wall. The more particles in the blood, the more get trapped. It's a numbers game.
3. **The immune system overreacts.** Trapped particles get chemically modified (oxidised). Immune cells rush in, swallow them and swell into **foam cells** ([[06 - The Immune System & Inflammation]]). These die and release more fat and inflammatory signals. The plaque grows a soft, fatty, inflamed core.
4. **A cap forms.** The body covers the core with a fibrous cap, like a scab.
5. **Rupture.** If the cap cracks, the blood meets the plaque's contents and forms a clot within minutes. If the clot blocks the artery, that's a heart attack.

The crucial insight from step 5: **most heart attacks are caused by plaques that weren't severely narrowing the artery.** Soft, inflamed plaques that block only part of the artery are the ones most likely to rupture. So "no symptoms on a walk" or even "my stress test was fine" doesn't rule out risk. Symptoms appear only when an artery is badly narrowed; rupture can happen without any warning narrowing.

## 3. The real risk factors, in rough order of weight

The global **INTERHEART** study (which included Indian patients) found that a small set of measurable factors explained around 90% of heart attack risk worldwide. Adapted for an Indian family:

### Particle count: LDL, ApoB, non-HDL
The more ApoB particles, the faster plaque builds. LDL cholesterol on a report is a proxy for this; **ApoB** is a more direct measure; **non-HDL cholesterol** (total cholesterol minus HDL) is a free proxy calculated from a standard lipid panel. The relationship is cumulative: decades of moderately high LDL can do as much damage as a few years of very high LDL. Lower for longer is better.

### Blood pressure
High pressure batters the artery lining and strains the heart. It's the single largest contributor to stroke and a top contributor to heart attacks, and it has **no symptoms**. It doesn't show up on any blood test. Only a BP cuff finds it ([[20 - Blood Pressure, Stroke & Kidney Disease]]).

### Tobacco in any form
Smoking, beedi, gutkha, khaini and chewing tobacco all damage the lining, make blood more likely to clot and narrow arteries. Smokeless tobacco is not a "safe" alternative. Quitting helps fast: heart attack risk falls substantially within the first year ([[16 - Tobacco, Alcohol & Environmental Exposures]]).

### Diabetes and insulin resistance
High sugar damages the lining and insulin resistance produces the high-triglyceride, low-HDL, small-dense-LDL pattern ([[18 - Blood Sugar, Insulin Resistance & Diabetes]]). Diabetes roughly doubles heart risk.

### Waist and visceral fat
A large waist predicts heart attacks better than BMI in South Asians.

### Psychosocial stress, inactivity, diet, alcohol
All measurable contributors in INTERHEART. Daily fruit and vegetables and regular activity were protective.

### Lp(a): the genetic wildcard
About a quarter of South Asians have high Lp(a). Its level is set almost entirely by genes, so it's roughly the same your whole life, and diet and exercise barely change it. Ordinary statins don't lower it either. It's an independent cause of heart attacks, strokes and narrowing of the aortic valve.

High Lp(a) is still **actionable**: it means every other factor needs to be controlled more strictly (lower LDL targets, tighter BP, zero tobacco). It also means the person's children and siblings should test, since it's inherited. Everyone should have Lp(a) measured **once in their life** ([[27 - The Testing Calendar]]).

### Air pollution
Fine particle pollution (**PM2.5**, particles small enough to enter the bloodstream) and smoke from cooking with wood or dung inflame arteries. This is a real, under-discussed contributor in India.

## 4. Why the "low oil, still got a heart attack" story makes sense

Take a common Indian profile: boiled vegetables, very little oil, an active day of chores, not overweight. What's missing?

- **The plate is mostly refined carbohydrate.** Eight to ten rotis or rice twice a day with little protein feeds insulin resistance and high triglycerides, which build plaque just as surely as saturated fat.
- **Tobacco.** Chewing tobacco and gutkha are extremely common among older rural Indians and often not mentioned to doctors because "it isn't smoking."
- **BP is never measured.** Many people have had high BP for 10–20 years without knowing.
- **Genes.** High Lp(a) or a family tendency to high LDL can't be outrun by diet.
- **Activity without intensity or muscle.** Chores are valuable movement, but heart fitness and muscle mass need some harder effort ([[12 - Strength Training - Muscle as Medicine]], [[13 - Cardio, Mobility & Balance]]).

Oil is not irrelevant. **Trans fats** (from vanaspati and reused, repeatedly heated frying oil) are genuinely harmful, and swapping saturated fats for unsaturated ones helps LDL. But cutting oil to near zero while eating mostly refined grains often makes the overall picture worse, not better ([[11 - The Indian Plate - Rebuilding the Thali]]).

## 5. South Asians get it earlier and worse

South Asians tend to have heart attacks around a decade earlier than Europeans, often with disease in several arteries at once. The reasons stack: more insulin resistance at the same weight, the high-triglyceride, low-HDL pattern, more high Lp(a), more tobacco and later diagnosis.

Practical consequence: standard risk calculators built on Western data often **underestimate** risk for Indians. Being of South Asian descent is formally recognised as a "risk-enhancing factor" in US guidelines. Indian cardiology groups such as the Lipid Association of India recommend stricter LDL targets for most Indian adults than Western defaults.

## 6. Treatments: stents, bypass, medicines

When arteries are already badly narrowed:

- **Angioplasty with a stent.** A thin tube is threaded into the artery, a balloon widens the narrowing, and a small mesh tube (**stent**) holds it open. Lifesaving during a heart attack.
- **Bypass surgery** (**CABG**, coronary artery bypass grafting). A blood vessel from elsewhere in the body is used to reroute blood around blockages. Often preferred when several arteries are involved, especially with diabetes.

Neither one cures atherosclerosis. They fix the worst spots; plaque keeps building everywhere else unless the risk factors are controlled. That's why after any stent or bypass, the medicines matter as much as the procedure:

- **Statins** lower LDL by making the liver pull more LDL out of the blood. They also stabilise plaques, making rupture less likely. Decades of large trials show they reduce heart attacks and deaths.
- **Antiplatelet drugs** such as aspirin make blood less likely to clot on a plaque. After a stent, these are critical; stopping them early can cause the stent to clot.
- **BP medicines** and, where needed, diabetes medicines.

Myths around these medicines (statins "damage the liver," "once you start you can never stop") stop many Indians from taking them. [[28 - Medicines Without Myths]] covers them properly.

## 7. Knowing your risk: what to measure

For any Indian adult, roughly from age 30 (earlier with family history):

- **Blood pressure**, at home, regularly.
- **Lipid profile** yearly: LDL, HDL, triglycerides, non-HDL. **ApoB** if available.
- **Lp(a)** once in life.
- **HbA1c and fasting sugar** yearly.
- **Waist** monthly.
- **hs-CRP** (high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, a marker of low-level inflammation) as a supporting signal. It's affected by any recent infection, so a single high value needs repeating.

For people with intermediate risk where the decision to start a statin is unclear, a **CT coronary calcium score** (a quick, low-dose CT scan that detects calcified plaque in the heart arteries) helps decide. Calcium means plaque is definitely present. A score of zero in middle age is reassuring, though it doesn't rule out soft plaque, especially in smokers.

**Family history** is a test too. A parent or sibling with a heart attack before 55 (men) or 65 (women) is a major warning sign for everyone else in the family.

---

## The layer, in one line

Heart attacks happen when an inflamed plaque inside an artery wall ruptures and clots; plaque is built by too many ApoB particles slipping through a lining damaged by high BP, high sugar and tobacco, so the levers are particle count, pressure, sugar, tobacco and genes, not oil alone.
