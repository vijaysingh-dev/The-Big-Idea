# Micronutrients & RDAs

*Health & Fitness · 2026-09-26*

The [[01 - Human Body & Nutrition Basics]] note covered macronutrients — the stuff you need in bulk for energy and structure. This note covers the small-quantity stuff that makes all of that machinery actually run: vitamins, minerals, and the numbers ("RDAs") that tell you how much you need. If macronutrients are the bricks and mortar of the body, micronutrients are the tools and electricians needed to actually assemble them.

---

## 1. Micronutrients don't build you — they let you build yourself

**Micronutrients** — vitamins and minerals — are needed in small amounts (milligrams or even micrograms, versus the tens or hundreds of grams of macronutrients), but they're not optional extras. Almost none of them provide energy directly (a common misconception — vitamins have zero calories). Instead, they act mostly as **cofactors** and **coenzymes**: small helper molecules that enzymes need to actually do their jobs.

An analogy that sticks: if a metabolic reaction is an assembly line, the macronutrient is the raw material going down the line, and the micronutrient is often the specific tool or badge a worker needs to be allowed to do their one step. Without it, the raw material just sits there, or the line grinds to a partial stop. This is why deficiencies rarely show up as "no energy" in a simple sense — they show up as specific, oddly targeted failures: wounds that don't heal, blood that doesn't clot properly, nerves that misfire.

Micronutrients split into two broad families:

- **Vitamins** — organic compounds (carbon-based), further split into:
  - **Fat-soluble** (A, D, E, K) — absorbed along with dietary fat, stored in fat tissue and the liver, so they can build up to toxic levels if wildly overdosed (a real risk with supplements, an almost nonexistent one from food alone).
  - **Water-soluble** (C and the B-vitamins) — not stored in any meaningful amount, so excess is simply flushed out in urine. This is why very high-dose vitamin C or B supplements mostly just produce expensive urine rather than lasting stores, and why water-soluble deficiencies show up faster if intake stops.
- **Minerals** — inorganic elements (not carbon-based), like calcium, iron, magnesium, potassium, and zinc, further split into **macrominerals** (needed in larger amounts — calcium, magnesium, potassium, sodium) and **trace minerals** (tiny amounts — iron, zinc, iodine, selenium).

## 2. RDAs are a floor, not a target, and definitely not a science of individuals

**RDA (Recommended Dietary Allowance)** is the daily intake level estimated to meet the needs of 97–98% of healthy people in a given age and sex group. Two things about that definition matter more than the number itself:

- It's a **population** statistic, not an individual one. It's set deliberately high — usually average requirement plus a safety margin of roughly two standard deviations — precisely because individual need varies and nobody knows *your* exact requirement without testing. So an RDA is closer to "very unlikely to be too low for you" than "the amount you personally need."
- **Requirement varies with life stage and circumstance** far more than most food labels suggest: pregnancy dramatically raises folate and iron needs; older adults absorb vitamin B12 and vitamin D less efficiently from the same food; smokers need more vitamin C because smoking depletes it faster; athletes training hard lose more electrolytes through sweat than a sedentary RDA accounts for.

There's also a related, less-cited number worth knowing: the **UL (Tolerable Upper Intake Level)** — the highest daily intake unlikely to cause harm. For fat-soluble vitamins and several minerals, the gap between "not enough" and "too much" is narrower than people assume, which is the main argument against blindly stacking supplements "just in case."

## 3. A short list of the ones worth actually knowing

Not exhaustive, but these show up constantly in health conversations, and knowing *why* explains most of the headlines:

- **Vitamin D** — functions more like a hormone than a classic vitamin. Made in skin from sun exposure (UVB), and its main job is regulating calcium absorption for bone health, though it's also tied to immune function. Deficiency is extremely common in people who get little sun or have darker skin (which needs more UV exposure to synthesize the same amount), which is why it's one of the few micronutrients regularly recommended as a standalone supplement even in otherwise healthy diets.
- **Vitamin B12** — needed for nerve function and red blood cell formation, found almost exclusively in animal products, which is why it's the one supplement vegans and strict vegetarians are near-universally advised to take; deficiency can take years to show symptoms because the body stores several years' worth in the liver.
- **Iron** — core component of **hemoglobin**, the molecule in red blood cells that carries oxygen. Comes in two forms: **heme iron** (from animal sources, absorbed efficiently) and **non-heme iron** (from plants, absorbed far less efficiently — vitamin C alongside a plant-iron meal meaningfully boosts absorption, while tea or coffee with the same meal blunts it). Deficiency causes fatigue and is one of the most common nutrient deficiencies worldwide, especially in menstruating women.
- **Calcium & Magnesium** — work together, not independently. Calcium is essential for bone density and muscle contraction; magnesium is needed for over 300 enzymatic reactions, including the one that lets muscles *relax* after calcium tells them to contract — a reason magnesium deficiency is linked to cramping.
- **Potassium & Sodium** — the body's main **electrolytes** (minerals that carry electrical charge, essential for nerve signaling and muscle contraction, including the heart). They work in opposition to regulate fluid balance across cell membranes. Modern diets, especially processed-food-heavy ones, tend to run high sodium and low potassium relative to what human physiology evolved for — a ratio more strongly linked to blood pressure issues than sodium alone.
- **Zinc** — involved in immune function, wound healing, and testosterone production; a reason it shows up in "immune support" and "test booster" supplements alike, though supplementing beyond an actual deficiency does little for someone who isn't deficient.

## 4. Food form beats supplement form, usually

A pattern worth internalizing rather than memorizing vitamin-by-vitamin: whole foods deliver micronutrients bundled with fiber, water, and hundreds of other plant compounds (**phytonutrients**) that appear to matter for absorption and effect in ways isolated supplements don't fully replicate. This is sometimes called the **food matrix effect** — beta-carotene from a carrot and beta-carotene from a pill behave differently in the body, and large trials isolating single antioxidant supplements (like beta-carotene or vitamin E in high doses) have occasionally shown *neutral or even negative* outcomes compared to getting the same nutrient from food.

This doesn't make supplements useless — they're a genuinely effective, low-cost fix for a specific, identified gap (vitamin D in low-sun climates, B12 for vegans, iron for a diagnosed deficiency). It does mean "eat a wide variety of whole foods" quietly outperforms "take a multivitamin" as a general strategy, and that supplementation works best as a targeted patch, not a blanket substitute for diet.

---

## The layer, in one line

Macronutrients give you energy and building material; micronutrients are what let the body actually use that material correctly. RDAs are a population safety floor, not a personal prescription, and the gap between too little and too much is narrower than food labels suggest — which is why "eat varied whole foods, supplement specific known gaps" beats both "don't worry about it" and "take everything."
