# Blood, Circulation & the Heart

*Health & Fitness · 2026-10-30*

Every cell in the body is at most a hair's width from a blood vessel, because every cell needs a constant delivery of oxygen and fuel and a constant removal of waste. The heart, the vessels and the blood itself form that delivery network, and most of the numbers on a routine blood report are measurements of how well it's working.

---

## 1. What blood is made of

An adult has about 5 litres of blood. Spin a sample in a tube and it separates into layers:

- **Plasma (about 55%):** a yellowish fluid, mostly water, carrying dissolved glucose, fats and lipoproteins, proteins such as albumin and clotting factors, hormones ([[04 - Hormones - The Body's Messaging System]]), salts (sodium, potassium, calcium) and waste products like urea and creatinine. Most "blood chemistry" tests measure things in plasma or **serum** (plasma after the clotting proteins are removed).
- **Red blood cells (about 40–45%).**
- **White blood cells and platelets (under 1%).**

### Red blood cells: oxygen delivery
**Red blood cells** (RBCs) are packed with **haemoglobin** (Hb), an iron-containing protein that grabs oxygen in the lungs and releases it in the tissues. RBCs are made in the bone marrow, live about 120 days, and are then recycled by the spleen and liver.

Making them requires **iron**, **vitamin B12** and **folate** ([[02 - Micronutrients & RDAs]]), plus the kidney hormone **EPO**. A shortage of any of these causes **anaemia** — too little haemoglobin to carry enough oxygen. Symptoms are tiredness, breathlessness on effort, pale skin and palpitations. Anaemia is extremely common in India, especially among women and children ([[22 - Thyroid, Anaemia & Common Deficiencies]]).

### White blood cells: defence
**White blood cells** (WBCs) are the immune system's mobile cells. A blood count breaks them into types — neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils and basophils — each with a different job. [[06 - The Immune System & Inflammation]] covers them.

### Platelets: clotting
**Platelets** are cell fragments that rush to a damaged vessel, stick together and start a clot. Clotting is essential for stopping bleeding, but the same process causes heart attacks and strokes when a clot forms on a ruptured plaque inside an artery ([[19 - How Heart Disease Actually Happens]]). Aspirin and similar drugs work by making platelets less sticky.

**Dengue** is dangerous largely because it can drop the platelet count very low and make blood vessels leaky ([[25 - Fast Killers - Emergencies & Warning Signs]]).

## 2. Reading the CBC

The **CBC** (complete blood count, also called a hemogram) is the most common blood test. Key values:

| Value | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **Haemoglobin (Hb)** | Oxygen-carrying capacity. Low = anaemia. |
| **Haematocrit (PCV)** | The percentage of blood made up of red cells. |
| **RBC count** | Number of red cells. |
| **MCV** (mean corpuscular volume) | Average size of red cells. **Small** suggests iron deficiency or thalassaemia trait; **large** suggests B12 or folate deficiency. |
| **MCH / MCHC** | How much haemoglobin is in each cell. Low means pale, under-filled cells, often from iron deficiency. |
| **RDW** (red cell distribution width) | How much red cell sizes vary. High often means a deficiency is developing, or a mix of causes. |
| **Total WBC and the differential** | The number and mix of white cells; a clue to infection, allergy or parasites. |
| **Platelet count** | Clotting cells. |
| **MPV, PDW** | Platelet size and variation; minor supporting markers. |

A CBC is cheap, widely available, and often the first place where deficiencies, infections and occasionally blood cancers show up.

## 3. The heart: a four-chambered double pump

The heart is a fist-sized muscle in the centre of the chest, tilted slightly left. It's really **two pumps side by side**:

- The **right side** receives used, low-oxygen blood from the body and pumps it to the **lungs** to pick up oxygen and get rid of carbon dioxide.
- The **left side** receives oxygen-rich blood from the lungs and pumps it out to the **whole body**. Because it has to push harder, the left side's main pumping chamber, the **left ventricle**, has the thickest muscle.

Each side has two chambers:
- An **atrium** (upper, receiving chamber)
- A **ventricle** (lower, pumping chamber)

**Valves** between the chambers and at the exits work like one-way doors, keeping blood flowing forward. The "lub-dub" of a heartbeat is the sound of valves closing. Damaged or narrowed valves are a separate kind of heart disease; in India, **rheumatic heart disease** (valve damage that can follow untreated strep throat infections in childhood) is still seen, and narrowing of the aortic valve becomes more common with age.

The heart muscle gets its own blood supply from the **coronary arteries**, the vessels that get blocked in a heart attack ([[19 - How Heart Disease Actually Happens]]).

## 4. The heart's electrical system

Each heartbeat starts with an electrical impulse from a natural pacemaker in the right atrium called the **SA node** (sinoatrial node). The signal spreads across the atria, pauses briefly at a relay point (the **AV node**), then spreads through the ventricles, making them contract in a coordinated squeeze.

An **ECG** (electrocardiogram) records this electrical activity through stickers on the chest. It's cheap, quick and painless, and shows:
- Heart rate and rhythm
- Signs of a heart attack happening now or in the past
- Thickened heart muscle (often from long-term high BP)
- Rhythm problems

**Atrial fibrillation** (AF) is a common rhythm problem in older adults where the atria quiver instead of beating properly. It raises stroke risk sharply because clots can form in the quivering chamber. An irregular pulse, felt at the wrist, is worth getting checked.

Two other common heart tests:
- An **echocardiogram** ("echo") is an ultrasound of the heart. It shows the chambers, valves and how strongly the heart pumps. **Ejection fraction** (EF) is the percentage of blood the left ventricle pumps out with each beat, normally around 55–70%.
- A **TMT** (treadmill test, also called a stress test) records an ECG while walking on a treadmill, to see whether narrowed arteries starve the heart of blood during effort.

## 5. The vessels: arteries, capillaries, veins

- **Arteries** carry blood away from the heart. They have thick, elastic, muscular walls that absorb each pulse of pressure. The biggest is the **aorta**.
- **Capillaries** are tiny vessels, often just one cell thick, where oxygen, nutrients and waste are exchanged with tissues. Laid end to end, the body's capillaries would stretch tens of thousands of kilometres.
- **Veins** carry blood back to the heart at low pressure. They have one-way valves, and walking helps because calf muscles squeeze the leg veins and push blood upward. This is why long periods of sitting, such as long journeys, raise the risk of leg clots.

The thin inner lining of every vessel, the **endothelium**, is a living, active layer, not just a pipe surface. It releases signals (such as **nitric oxide**) that relax vessels and prevent clots. High BP, high sugar, tobacco and inflammation damage it, which is the first step of atherosclerosis.

## 6. Blood pressure, simply explained

**Blood pressure** (BP) is the force of blood against artery walls. It's written as two numbers:

- **Systolic** (top number): the pressure when the heart contracts.
- **Diastolic** (bottom number): the pressure when the heart relaxes between beats.

The unit is millimetres of mercury (mmHg). Around **120/80 or lower** is considered healthy for most adults. Higher readings that persist count as high BP, or **hypertension**. Exact thresholds and treatment targets differ slightly between guidelines and are covered in [[20 - Blood Pressure, Stroke & Kidney Disease]].

BP rises and falls through the day with activity, stress, caffeine and posture. A single high reading at a clinic doesn't make a diagnosis, and anxiety at the doctor's office ("white-coat hypertension") can push readings up. Home readings, taken properly and repeated over days, give a truer picture.

Long-term high BP is dangerous because it:
- Damages the artery lining, speeding atherosclerosis
- Forces the heart to work harder, thickening and eventually weakening it
- Damages small vessels in the kidneys, eyes and brain
- Can burst weakened vessels in the brain (haemorrhagic stroke)

And it usually has **no symptoms at all** until damage is done.

## 7. Heart rate and fitness

**Resting heart rate** for most adults is 60–100 beats per minute; fit people are often lower, in the 50s or 60s, because a stronger heart pumps more blood with each beat. A resting heart rate that's consistently high can reflect poor fitness, anaemia, an overactive thyroid, dehydration, stress or illness.

**Cardiorespiratory fitness** — how well the heart, lungs and muscles work together to use oxygen during effort — is one of the strongest predictors of long life. It's often measured as **VO2max** (the maximum rate the body can use oxygen). It can be improved at any age with regular aerobic exercise ([[13 - Cardio, Mobility & Balance]]).

## 8. Everyday signs the circulation needs attention

- Breathlessness or chest discomfort on effort that eases with rest
- Swelling of both ankles that's new or getting worse
- Calf pain when walking that eases with rest (possible narrowed leg arteries)
- An irregular or very fast pulse at rest
- Fainting or near-fainting
- Unusual tiredness, especially with pale skin (possible anaemia)

Sudden chest pain, breathlessness, weakness on one side, or slurred speech are emergencies ([[25 - Fast Killers - Emergencies & Warning Signs]]).

---

## The layer, in one line

Blood is plasma carrying red cells for oxygen, white cells for defence and platelets for clotting; the heart is two pumps driven by its own electrical system and fed by its own small arteries, and blood pressure is the force on every vessel — silent when high, but shaping the health of the heart, brain and kidneys for decades.
