# Energy Metabolism: How the Body Makes and Uses Fuel

*Health & Fitness · 2026-10-30*

Every heartbeat, thought and step is paid for in one currency, a molecule called ATP. How your body makes that currency, which fuel it draws on, where it stores the leftovers and how it switches between them explains most of what happens with weight, blood sugar, fatigue and exercise.

---

## 1. ATP: the only currency cells accept

[[01 - Human Body & Nutrition Basics]] covered energy balance: calories in versus calories out. This note looks inside that equation.

Food doesn't power cells directly. Carbohydrate, fat and protein are first broken down, and their energy is used to make **ATP** (adenosine triphosphate). ATP is a small molecule that releases energy when one of its phosphate groups is snapped off. Muscle contraction, nerve signals and building new proteins all run on ATP.

The body keeps only a tiny stock of ATP at any moment, enough for a few seconds of hard effort. So it has to be remade constantly. An adult recycles roughly their own body weight in ATP every day. **Metabolism** is the collective name for all the chemical reactions that do this, plus the reactions that build and store things.

## 2. Mitochondria: the power plants

Most ATP is made inside **mitochondria**, small structures inside cells that use oxygen to fully burn fuel. This is **aerobic** (oxygen-using) metabolism. It's slow to ramp up but enormously efficient: one glucose molecule burned completely yields roughly 30 ATP.

Muscle cells are packed with mitochondria, and training increases their number and efficiency. This is a big part of why fit people tire less and why exercise improves blood sugar: more mitochondria means more capacity to burn glucose and fat. Inactivity, ageing and insulin resistance all go along with fewer, less efficient mitochondria.

## 3. Three energy systems, by speed

The body has three ways of making ATP, and they overlap rather than switching on and off:

| System | Fuel | Speed | Lasts | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Phosphocreatine** | Creatine phosphate stored in muscle | Instant | ~10 seconds | Lifting a heavy sack, a sprint |
| **Glycolysis** (anaerobic) | Glucose/glycogen, no oxygen needed | Fast | ~30 seconds to 2 minutes | Climbing stairs quickly, a hard set of squats |
| **Aerobic** | Glucose and fat, with oxygen | Slow to start | Hours | Walking, farm work, cycling |

Anaerobic glycolysis produces **lactate** as a by-product. Lactate isn't the cause of muscle soreness the next day (that's micro-damage, covered in note 01) and is actually recycled as fuel. The burning feeling during hard effort is linked to the acidity that builds up alongside it.

**Practical point:** strength training mainly works the fast systems; walking mainly works the aerobic system. A healthy body needs both, which is why [[12 - Strength Training - Muscle as Medicine]] and [[13 - Cardio, Mobility & Balance]] are separate notes.

## 4. Where fuel is stored

| Store | Where | Roughly how much | Lasts |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Blood glucose** | Bloodstream | ~4–5 g | Minutes |
| **Liver glycogen** | Liver | ~80–100 g | Keeps blood sugar steady overnight |
| **Muscle glycogen** | Muscles | ~300–500 g | Used only by that muscle |
| **Body fat** | Under the skin and around organs | Many kilograms | Weeks |
| **Protein** | Mainly muscle | Not a store; it's working tissue | Broken down only when needed |

**Glycogen** is glucose packed into a compact, branched chain. Liver glycogen is shared with the whole body; muscle glycogen is selfish and used only by the muscle that holds it.

**Fat** is the big reserve. Even a lean adult carries enough fat to fuel weeks of basic needs. Fat is dense, giving about 9 calories per gram against about 4 for carbohydrate and protein.

**Protein** is not meant to be fuel. It's used mainly for building and repair. The body breaks muscle down for fuel only under strain: prolonged fasting, starvation, serious illness, or very low protein intake. This is why crash diets and illness cost muscle.

## 5. Fed state and fasted state

The body cycles between two modes every day, coordinated by hormones ([[04 - Hormones - The Body's Messaging System]]):

### Fed state (the few hours after a meal)
- Blood glucose rises and **insulin** rises with it.
- Cells take in glucose and burn it as the main fuel.
- Spare glucose tops up glycogen; once glycogen is full, the excess is turned into fat in the liver and stored.
- Fat burning is switched down, because insulin says "store, don't burn."

### Fasted state (overnight, between meals)
- Insulin falls, and **glucagon** rises.
- The liver releases glucose from glycogen to keep blood sugar steady.
- Fat cells release fatty acids, which muscles and most organs burn.
- After a longer fast (usually 12–24 hours or more, depending on activity), the liver starts making **ketones** from fat, an alternative fuel the brain can use. This is the basis of ketogenic diets and fasting, covered in note 01.

**Metabolic flexibility** is the ability to switch smoothly between burning glucose and burning fat depending on what's available. Healthy, active people are flexible. People with insulin resistance are less so: their insulin stays high, fat burning stays switched off, and fat keeps accumulating even between meals ([[18 - Blood Sugar, Insulin Resistance & Diabetes]]).

## 6. Fuel mix during activity

The body always burns a mix of fat and carbohydrate. The ratio depends on intensity:

- **Rest and easy activity** (sitting, slow walking): mostly fat.
- **Moderate effort** (brisk walking, farm work): a mix, with fat still a large share.
- **Hard effort** (running uphill, heavy lifting): mostly carbohydrate, because it can be burned faster.

This leads to a common myth: that slow exercise is better for fat loss because a larger *percentage* of the energy comes from fat. What matters for body fat over weeks is total energy balance and muscle preservation, not which fuel was burned in a given hour. Both moderate and harder exercise work; harder exercise also builds fitness faster.

## 7. Where the daily calories go

Daily energy use (**TDEE**, total daily energy expenditure) has four parts:

1. **BMR** (basal metabolic rate): energy for keeping you alive at rest — heart, brain, liver, kidneys, breathing, body temperature. Usually the biggest share, around 60–70% for most people.
2. **TEF** (thermic effect of food): energy used to digest food, about 10%. Protein has the highest TEF, which is one reason high-protein meals keep people fuller.
3. **NEAT** (non-exercise activity thermogenesis): everyday movement that isn't formal exercise — walking around, chores, standing, farm work. It varies hugely between people and is a big reason some people stay lean.
4. **Exercise**: deliberate training. Often a smaller share than people think.

### What happens with age
A large 2021 study in *Science* (Pontzer and colleagues), using precise measurements on thousands of people, found that metabolism adjusted for body size stays remarkably stable from about age 20 to 60 and declines only slowly after 60. The "my metabolism slowed down at 40" feeling is mostly due to **losing muscle and moving less**, not an unavoidable metabolic crash. Muscle is metabolically active tissue; keeping it keeps BMR up.

### What happens with dieting
Cutting calories sharply triggers **adaptive thermogenesis** (covered in note 01): the body spends less energy, partly by reducing NEAT and partly through hormonal changes. Combined with muscle loss when protein is low, this is why crash diets backfire. Moderate deficits, enough protein, and strength training preserve muscle and metabolism.

## 8. Why this matters for an Indian family

- **Big carbohydrate meals keep the body in "store" mode.** Eating large grain-heavy meals two or three times a day, plus sugary chai in between, keeps insulin high for much of the day, with little time spent burning fat ([[11 - The Indian Plate - Rebuilding the Thali]]).
- **Daily movement (NEAT) is valuable but has limits.** Farm work and chores burn real energy, but they don't build much muscle or aerobic fitness on their own.
- **Muscle is the metabolic engine.** More muscle means more mitochondria, more glucose storage, a higher BMR and better metabolic flexibility.
- **Fatigue is often metabolic.** Persistent tiredness can come from anaemia (less oxygen delivered to mitochondria, [[05 - Blood, Circulation & the Heart]]), an underactive thyroid (which slows the whole metabolic rate, [[04 - Hormones - The Body's Messaging System]]), poor sleep, or high blood sugar. It's worth testing rather than assuming it's age.

---

## The layer, in one line

Cells run on ATP made mostly by mitochondria from glucose and fat; the body stores a little glucose as glycogen and a lot of energy as fat, shifts between "store" and "burn" with each meal and fast, and its energy budget depends mostly on how much muscle it carries and how much it moves.
