# Hormones: The Body's Messaging System

*Health & Fitness · 2026-10-30*

Hunger, sleep, blood sugar, mood, growth, fertility and the speed at which you burn energy are all set by a few dozen chemical messengers released in tiny amounts into the blood. When one of them goes slightly off, the effects show up everywhere, which is why a thyroid problem can look like laziness and a stress problem can look like diabetes.

---

## 1. What a hormone is

A **hormone** is a chemical messenger made by a gland or organ and carried in the blood to distant cells, where it changes what those cells do. The system of hormone-producing glands is called the **endocrine system**.

Three ideas make hormones easier to understand:

- **Receptors decide who listens.** A hormone affects only cells that carry its matching receptor, like a radio station heard only by radios tuned to it. Insulin resistance ([[18 - Blood Sugar, Insulin Resistance & Diabetes]]) is a case of cells "turning down the volume" on their receptors' signal.
- **Tiny amounts, big effects.** Hormones circulate at minute concentrations, so small changes matter. This is why reference ranges on reports are narrow.
- **Feedback loops keep things steady.** Most hormones are controlled like a thermostat: when a level rises, the gland that triggered it is told to slow down. This **negative feedback** keeps the body stable.

Nerves send fast, targeted signals (milliseconds). Hormones send slower, broadcast signals (minutes to days). The two systems work together ([[10 - The Brain & Nervous System]]).

## 2. The master control: hypothalamus and pituitary

At the base of the brain sit two small structures that run much of the endocrine system:

- The **hypothalamus** monitors the body (temperature, hunger, stress, water balance) and sends instructions.
- The **pituitary gland**, just below it, is the "master gland." It releases hormones that tell other glands what to do.

This creates a chain of command: hypothalamus → pituitary → target gland → hormone that acts on the body, with feedback back up the chain.

**Thyroid example (important for reading reports):**
1. When the hypothalamus senses that more thyroid hormone is needed, it signals the pituitary.
2. The pituitary releases **TSH** (thyroid-stimulating hormone).
3. TSH tells the thyroid gland in the neck to make **T4** (thyroxine) and some **T3** (triiodothyronine, the more active form).
4. When T4 and T3 are high enough, they signal the pituitary to release less TSH.

So a **high TSH** usually means the thyroid is underperforming and the pituitary is "shouting" at it to work harder. A **low TSH** usually means too much thyroid hormone. TSH often moves before T4 does, which is why a high TSH with a normal T4 (called **subclinical hypothyroidism**) can be an early warning ([[22 - Thyroid, Anaemia & Common Deficiencies]]).

## 3. The key hormones and what they do

### Metabolism and fuel
- **Insulin** (pancreas): lowers blood sugar by moving glucose into cells; promotes storage of glycogen and fat ([[03 - Energy Metabolism - How the Body Makes and Uses Fuel]]).
- **Glucagon** (pancreas): raises blood sugar by telling the liver to release glucose between meals.
- **Thyroid hormones T4 and T3** (thyroid): set the body's overall metabolic speed. Too little: tiredness, weight gain, feeling cold, constipation, dry skin, slowed thinking, high cholesterol. Too much: weight loss, fast heartbeat, tremor, anxiety, heat intolerance.

### Stress
- **Cortisol** (adrenal glands, which sit on top of the kidneys): the long-acting stress hormone. It raises blood sugar, mobilises energy, damps down inflammation and follows a daily rhythm, highest in the morning to help you wake up. Chronically high cortisol from ongoing stress, poor sleep, or long-term steroid medicines raises blood sugar, blood pressure and belly fat and weakens muscle and bone ([[15 - Stress, Mental Health & Social Connection]]).
- **Adrenaline** (also adrenal glands): the instant "fight or flight" hormone. Raises heart rate and blood pressure in seconds.

### Hunger and fullness
- **Ghrelin** (stomach): the hunger hormone. Rises before meals and after poor sleep.
- **Leptin** (fat tissue): signals to the brain that energy stores are sufficient. People with a lot of body fat often become **leptin resistant**, so the "you're full" signal doesn't register properly.
- **GLP-1** (gut): released after eating; slows stomach emptying, increases insulin and signals fullness. The newer weight-loss and diabetes medicines (such as semaglutide) mimic this hormone ([[28 - Medicines Without Myths]]).

Protein and fibre trigger fullness hormones more strongly than refined carbohydrate, which is part of why the same calories from rotis alone leave people hungrier than a meal with dal and sabzi ([[11 - The Indian Plate - Rebuilding the Thali]]).

### Growth and repair
- **Growth hormone** (pituitary): released mainly during deep sleep; supports repair, muscle and bone.
- **Testosterone** (mainly testes in men, small amounts in women): supports muscle, bone, red blood cell production and libido. Gradually declines with age, and falls faster with obesity, poor sleep and diabetes.
- **Oestrogen** (mainly ovaries in women before menopause): protects bone and has favourable effects on cholesterol and blood vessels. Its fall at menopause is why women's bone loss speeds up and heart risk rises afterwards ([[29 - Health Across Life Stages]]).

### Water, salt and blood pressure
- **ADH** (antidiuretic hormone, from the pituitary): tells the kidneys to hold water.
- **Renin and aldosterone** (kidneys and adrenals): control salt retention and blood pressure ([[08 - The Liver & Kidneys - Factories and Filters]]).

### Bones and calcium
- **PTH** (parathyroid hormone, from four tiny glands next to the thyroid): raises blood calcium by pulling it from bone and increasing absorption.
- **Vitamin D** behaves like a hormone once activated by the liver and kidneys: it lets the gut absorb calcium. Deficiency is very common in India despite the sunshine ([[02 - Micronutrients & RDAs]]).

### Sleep and daily rhythm
- **Melatonin** (pineal gland in the brain): rises in darkness and signals that it's night. Bright light at night, especially from screens, suppresses it ([[14 - Sleep & Circadian Rhythm]]).

### Blood production
- **EPO** (erythropoietin, from the kidneys): tells the bone marrow to make red blood cells. Kidney disease causes anaemia partly because EPO falls.

## 4. Hormones are a network

Hormones constantly influence each other, so one problem often shows up as several:

- **Poor sleep** raises cortisol and ghrelin and lowers insulin sensitivity: hungrier, higher blood sugar, more belly fat.
- **Excess belly fat** worsens insulin resistance and leptin resistance, and in men lowers testosterone.
- **Underactive thyroid** raises LDL cholesterol and causes weight gain and tiredness. Before starting a statin, doctors often check TSH.
- **Chronic stress** raises cortisol, which raises blood sugar and blood pressure.
- **Menopause** lowers oestrogen, which speeds bone loss and shifts fat towards the belly.

This network view explains why lifestyle levers have such wide effects: better sleep, less belly fat and regular exercise improve many hormones at once.

## 5. Common hormone problems in India

- **Thyroid disorders** are very common, especially **hypothyroidism** (underactive thyroid) in women. A TSH test is cheap and worth doing every year or two for adults, and more often for women.
- **Type 2 diabetes** is fundamentally a hormone problem: insulin resistance plus failing insulin production ([[18 - Blood Sugar, Insulin Resistance & Diabetes]]).
- **PCOS** (polycystic ovary syndrome) affects many young Indian women. It involves insulin resistance and high male hormones, causing irregular periods, acne, excess hair and difficulty losing weight. It also raises future diabetes risk.
- **Vitamin D deficiency** is widespread across all ages.
- **Steroid misuse** is a real issue. Some unregulated "ayurvedic" or quick-relief medicines for joint pain or asthma have been found to contain hidden steroids, which can cause diabetes, high BP, bone loss and dangerous suppression of the body's own cortisol.

## 6. How hormones are tested

- **TSH** (with T4 if abnormal): thyroid.
- **HbA1c and fasting glucose**: blood sugar control. Fasting insulin occasionally.
- **Vitamin D (25-OH vitamin D)**: vitamin D status.
- **Testosterone, oestrogen, LH, FSH, prolactin**: only when there are specific symptoms (fertility problems, irregular periods, low libido).
- **Cortisol**: only when a doctor suspects a specific disorder.

Timing matters for some hormones. Cortisol and testosterone vary through the day, so tests are usually done in the morning. TSH can be affected by acute illness, so an abnormal result during or right after a fever is usually rechecked.

---

## The layer, in one line

Hormones are slow, broadcast chemical signals controlled by feedback loops; a handful of them (insulin, thyroid hormones, cortisol, sex hormones, and the hunger signals) set metabolism, appetite, stress response and body composition, and because they're linked, sleep, belly fat and stress shift many of them at once.
