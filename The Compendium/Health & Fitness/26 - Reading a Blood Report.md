# Reading a Blood Report

*Health & Fitness · 2026-10-30*

A modern "full body checkup" report runs to 30 or 40 pages and flags a dozen values in bold. Most families either panic at every bold number or file the report away unread. Neither helps. Reading a report well is a skill: knowing what a reference range really means, which values matter, which patterns connect, and which flags are noise.

---

## 1. Five principles before you read a single number

### 1. A reference range is a statistical boundary, not a health boundary
Most reference ranges are set so that about **95% of apparently healthy people** fall within them. That means about **1 in 20 healthy people** will be "outside range" on any single test by chance. A 40-test panel will almost always flag something.

But the reverse is also true: for some tests, "normal" isn't the same as "optimal." Most Indian adults have LDL inside the lab's normal range and still get heart attacks; a fasting sugar of 99 is "normal" but close to prediabetes. **Some tests use risk-based targets**, not population ranges — LDL, HbA1c, BP, Lp(a) and hs-CRP among them.

### 2. Trends beat single values
A haemoglobin of 11.8 that was 12.8 last year tells a different story from one that's been 11.8 for a decade. Keep a log of key values over time ([[24 - Silent Killers - Diseases That Hide Until Late]]).

### 3. Patterns beat individual numbers
Values make sense together. Low haemoglobin plus small red cells plus high RDW suggests iron deficiency. High triglycerides plus low HDL plus high fasting sugar plus a large waist suggests insulin resistance. A single number rarely tells the whole story.

### 4. Context matters
- **Fasting:** glucose and triglycerides need 8–12 hours of fasting. A non-fasting sample can mislead.
- **Recent illness:** a cold or infection can raise CRP, hs-CRP, ferritin and white cells and temporarily disturb TSH.
- **Exercise:** heavy exercise the day before can raise AST, CK and creatinine.
- **Medicines and supplements:** biotin supplements can interfere with some hormone tests; statins, steroids, diuretics and others shift values.
- **Muscle mass:** creatinine depends on muscle, so it reads low in thin older adults even if kidney function is reduced.
- **Different labs, methods and units** give slightly different values; compare across years ideally using the same lab.

### 5. A report is a question, not a diagnosis
Abnormal results lead to a doctor's interpretation, often a repeat test, and sometimes further tests. Bring the report, your log of previous results, and your list of medicines to the doctor.

## 2. The blood count (CBC / hemogram)
Covered in detail in [[05 - Blood, Circulation & the Heart]] and [[22 - Thyroid, Anaemia & Common Deficiencies]].

| Value | Low may suggest | High may suggest |
|---|---|---|
| **Haemoglobin** | Anaemia (iron, B12, folate, chronic disease, blood loss) | Dehydration, smoking, lung disease, living at altitude, rarely a marrow disorder |
| **MCV** | Iron deficiency, thalassaemia trait | B12 or folate deficiency, alcohol, thyroid, liver disease |
| **MCHC / MCH** | Iron deficiency | Usually not significant |
| **RDW** | — | Developing deficiency, mixed causes |
| **WBC total** | Viral infection, some medicines, marrow problems | Infection, inflammation, stress, steroids |
| **Neutrophils** | Viral infection, medicines | Bacterial infection |
| **Lymphocytes** | Stress, steroids | Viral infection |
| **Eosinophils** | — | Worms, allergy, asthma, some skin conditions |
| **Monocytes** | Usually not significant alone | Chronic infection, recovery |
| **Basophils** | Usually not significant | Allergy, rare marrow disorders |
| **Platelets** | Dengue and other infections, some medicines, liver disease, lab clumping artefact | Iron deficiency, inflammation, rarely a marrow disorder |
| **MPV, PDW, PLCR** | — | Larger, younger platelets; minor supporting markers that rarely matter alone |

Machine counts are sometimes off; a **peripheral smear** (a doctor looking at blood under a microscope) clarifies unusual results. Remarks like "anisocytosis" (red cells of varying sizes) and "poikilocytosis" (varying shapes) usually point to a deficiency.

## 3. Blood sugar
Covered in [[18 - Blood Sugar, Insulin Resistance & Diabetes]].
- **Fasting glucose:** below 100 normal; 100–125 prediabetes; 126+ diabetes.
- **HbA1c:** below 5.7% normal; 5.7–6.4% prediabetes; 6.5%+ diabetes. For people being treated for diabetes, the usual target is below 7%, individualised by age and health.
- **Average blood glucose (eAG)** is just HbA1c converted into glucose units; it's not a separate measurement.
- **Urine glucose** appears only when blood sugar is high enough to spill over; a normal urine test doesn't exclude diabetes.

## 4. The lipid profile
Covered in [[19 - How Heart Disease Actually Happens]].
- **LDL cholesterol:** the main target. "Below 100" is the common lab range, but targets depend on risk: around below 70 for high-risk people (diabetes with other risk factors, high Lp(a), strong family history), and below 55 for very high risk (after a heart attack or stroke), according to many guidelines including the Lipid Association of India.
- **HDL:** below 40 (men) or 50 (women) is a warning sign, especially with high triglycerides.
- **Triglycerides:** below 150 desirable; high values signal insulin resistance, excess carbohydrate, alcohol or uncontrolled diabetes.
- **Non-HDL cholesterol** (total minus HDL): a good overall measure of harmful particles; target roughly 30 above the LDL target.
- **Ratios** (TC/HDL, LDL/HDL, TG/HDL): summarise the above. A high **TG/HDL ratio** is a useful marker of insulin resistance.
- **ApoB:** a direct count of harmful particles; useful when triglycerides are high or LDL seems "normal."
- **ApoA1 and the ApoB/ApoA1 ratio:** higher ratio means higher risk.
- **Lp(a):** below 30 mg/dL (about 75 nmol/L) is low risk; above 50 mg/dL (about 125 nmol/L) is high risk. Units vary by lab (mg/dL or nmol/L), so check before comparing. **Measure once.**

## 5. Liver panel (LFT)
Covered in [[08 - The Liver & Kidneys - Factories and Filters]].
- **ALT (SGPT):** the most liver-specific marker. Mild rises are common with fatty liver.
- **AST (SGOT):** liver and muscle.
- **AST/ALT ratio above 2** can suggest alcohol-related liver disease.
- **ALP:** bile duct problems or bone conditions.
- **GGT:** bile ducts, alcohol, some medicines.
- **Bilirubin:** jaundice. Isolated mildly high indirect bilirubin is often harmless Gilbert's syndrome.
- **Albumin:** low with poor liver function, malnutrition or kidney protein loss.
- **Globulin:** high with chronic inflammation or infection, rarely certain blood cancers.

Normal LFTs don't rule out fatty liver or early scarring.

## 6. Kidney panel (KFT) and electrolytes
Covered in [[08 - The Liver & Kidneys - Factories and Filters]] and [[20 - Blood Pressure, Stroke & Kidney Disease]].
- **Creatinine and eGFR:** eGFR above 90 normal; 60–89 mildly reduced (common with age); persistently below 60 means chronic kidney disease.
- **Slightly low creatinine** usually just reflects low muscle mass, not a kidney problem.
- **Urea / BUN:** affected by hydration and protein intake as well as kidneys. A **high BUN/creatinine ratio** often reflects dehydration, high protein intake, or low muscle mass rather than kidney disease.
- **Uric acid:** high values can cause gout and stones.
- **Sodium:** mildly low values (around 130–135 mmol/L) are common in older adults, often from medicines like diuretics, drinking large volumes of water, or vomiting; very low values are dangerous.
- **Potassium:** both high and low values can cause heart rhythm problems; ask about any abnormal result.
- **Calcium:** high or low values need follow-up; interpret with albumin.
- **Urine albumin (UACR):** the earliest sign of kidney damage; below 30 mg/g is normal.

## 7. Thyroid
Covered in [[22 - Thyroid, Anaemia & Common Deficiencies]].
- **TSH** is the first and most sensitive test. High TSH = underactive thyroid (if T4 low = overt; if T4 normal = subclinical). Low TSH = overactive.
- **Total T3 and T4** are affected by binding proteins (pregnancy, birth control pills); **free T4** is more reliable.
- **Repeat abnormal TSH** after 6–12 weeks before starting treatment, unless it's very abnormal or the person is pregnant.

## 8. Vitamins, minerals and iron
- **Vitamin D (25-OH):** below 20 deficient; 20–30 insufficient; 30–100 sufficient; very high levels risk toxicity.
- **Vitamin B12:** below about 200 pg/mL is usually deficient; low-normal values with symptoms may need further testing.
- **Folate:** low suggests poor intake.
- **Iron studies:** **ferritin** is the key iron-store measure (low confirms deficiency; it's raised by inflammation). Serum iron, TIBC, transferrin saturation and transferrin fill in the picture.
- **Zinc, magnesium, copper:** low zinc is common with grain-heavy vegetarian diets; interpret with a doctor rather than self-supplementing heavily.
- **Other B vitamins (B1, B2, B3, B5, B6, B7), vitamin A, E, K:** included in some premium packages. Values within the range are reassuring; borderline values rarely need treatment in a person eating a varied diet. These tests are of limited value for routine screening.

## 9. Inflammation and "special" markers
- **hs-CRP:** below 1 low cardiac risk, 1–3 average, above 3 higher; above 10 usually acute inflammation. Repeat a high value after 2–4 weeks ([[06 - The Immune System & Inflammation]]).
- **ESR:** non-specific; used mainly for specific conditions.
- **Tumour markers** (CEA, CA-125, CA 19-9, AFP): **not screening tests** for healthy people; non-cancer causes (including smoking) often raise them, and they can be normal in early cancer. Discuss any abnormal value with a doctor ([[23 - Cancer - How It Starts and How to Catch It Early]]).
- **Amylase and lipase:** pancreatic enzymes; mainly useful when someone has abdominal pain.
- **Heavy metals and trace elements panels** (lead, arsenic, cadmium, mercury, etc.): useful if exposure is suspected (groundwater, occupation, traditional medicines). Results within range are reassuring.

## 10. The urine routine
- **Protein, glucose, ketones, blood, nitrites, leucocytes (pus cells):** screen for kidney disease, uncontrolled diabetes, infection and bleeding.
- **Specific gravity:** hydration.
- **Normal urine routine doesn't exclude early kidney damage**; UACR is needed for that.

## 11. How to actually use a report

1. **Start with the summary page of out-of-range values**, but don't stop there.
2. **Group findings by system** — sugar, lipids, blood count, liver, kidney, thyroid, vitamins — and look for patterns.
3. **Separate three kinds of findings:**
   - **Act now:** values in clearly diseased ranges (for example, HbA1c 6.5%+, very high LDL, high Lp(a), eGFR below 60, very abnormal sodium or potassium, haemoglobin well below range).
   - **Recheck:** borderline or possibly transient values (slightly high hs-CRP, mildly abnormal TSH, slightly high bilirubin).
   - **Note and ignore:** minor deviations with no pattern (a slightly low monocyte count, a marginal PDW).
4. **Compare with previous reports.**
5. **Write questions** for the doctor before the visit.
6. **Note what's missing:** BP, waist, weight, eye checks and cancer screening don't come from a blood report but matter as much.

### Questions to ask the doctor
- Which of these results matter most for me, and why?
- Is anything a reason to start or change a medicine?
- What targets should I aim for (LDL, HbA1c, BP)?
- What should be repeated, and when?
- Is anything here a reason to test family members (for example, high Lp(a))?
- What would you change first in my lifestyle based on this?

---

## The layer, in one line

A blood report is a set of clues, not a verdict: reference ranges are statistical, trends and patterns matter more than single bold values, context such as fasting, recent illness and medicines changes meaning, some tests use risk targets rather than ranges, and every result should end in a clear action — act, recheck, or note and move on.
