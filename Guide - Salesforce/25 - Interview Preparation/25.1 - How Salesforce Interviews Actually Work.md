# How Salesforce Interviews Actually Work

## The rounds

Varies by company, and the shape is consistent.

```
Screen                  30 min    a recruiter, checking the basics
Technical               60 min    Apex, triggers, SOQL, limits
Scenario or design      60 min    "how would you build this"
Practical               60-90 min live coding, or a take-home
Behavioural             45 min    how you work
Final                   45 min    a hiring manager or architect
```

**Consultancies weight the scenario round.** They need people who can talk to a client and decide between a flow and a trigger.

**Product companies weight the coding round.** They need people who write maintainable Apex.

**End-user organizations weight the design round.** They need people who will not create the mess the last person left.

**Knowing which you are interviewing with changes what to prepare.**

## What is actually being assessed

Four things, and only the first is knowledge.

**Do you know the platform?** Order of execution, governor limits, sharing, the difference between a flow and a trigger.

**Do you reason about tradeoffs?** The strongest signal in any round. A candidate who says "it depends, and here is what it depends on" is more valuable than one who has memorized the answer.

**Have you actually built things?** Specific stories about specific problems, with the numbers.

**Would you be pleasant to work with?** Underrated, and it decides more outcomes than candidates expect.

## The signals that separate levels

Worth understanding, because the same question is asked at every level and the expected depth differs.

**Junior:** knows the syntax, knows the limits exist, can write a bulkified trigger.

**Mid:** knows why the limits exist, chooses between mechanisms deliberately, has debugged a production problem.

**Senior:** reasons about tradeoffs without being prompted, has an opinion informed by having been wrong, thinks about the team maintaining it in two years.

**Architect:** thinks about the org rather than the feature, considers governance and lifecycle, and is comfortable saying a requirement should be questioned.

**The tell for seniority is unprompted tradeoff discussion.** "I'd use a trigger, because the flow would need a query per record and this object saves in bulk from an integration" is a different answer from "a trigger".

## The technical round

**What comes up nearly always:**

```
Order of execution
Governor limits, and why they exist
Trigger design — one per object, handler pattern, bulkification
Flow versus Apex
Sharing and security in Apex
SOQL selectivity
Async — which mechanism, and why
Testing
```

**Order of execution is the most-asked question on the platform.** Knowing the sequence is table stakes; knowing what it implies is the signal.

```
Weak:   recites the sixteen steps
Strong: "Before triggers run at step 5, validation at 6 — so a before trigger
         can populate a field a validation rule requires. And after-save flows
         run after after-triggers, which is why a trigger can't see what a flow
         set. That's the concrete reason for not having both write the same field."
```

**Bulkification comes up in some form in every technical round**, usually as "what's wrong with this code".

**The follow-up that separates candidates:** "your trigger is perfectly bulkified and it still hits the query limit. Why?" The answer is the shared budget — flows, validation rules, roll-ups, and managed packages spending it before your code runs. See [[16.1 - Why Governor Limits Exist]].

## The scenario round

A business requirement, and you design the solution aloud.

```
"A manufacturer wants field engineers to submit warranty claims from a phone,
 with photos. Claims over ₹5 lakh need regional director approval. The ERP
 needs to know when a claim is approved. How would you build it?"
```

**What is being assessed:**

**Do you ask questions before answering?** Volume, users, existing systems, latency requirements. A candidate who starts designing immediately has skipped the most important part.

**Do you choose declarative where it fits?** Reaching for Apex when a screen flow and an approval process would do is a negative signal in most organizations.

**Do you consider volume?** "How many claims a day" changes the answer.

**Do you handle failure?** What happens when the ERP is down.

**Can you defend the choice?** And change it when given new information.

**The questions worth asking, in most scenarios:**

```
How many records, and how are they distributed?
Who uses this, and on what device?
What already exists that this should use?
What happens when the external system is unavailable?
Does this need to be real time?
Who maintains it — a developer, or an administrator?
```

**Asking three good questions before designing is a stronger opening than any answer.**

## The coding round

Live, or a take-home.

**What is assessed beyond correctness:**

```
Bulkification              non-negotiable
Naming                     readable, role-suffixed
Structure                  service layer, not everything in a trigger
Security                   sharing keyword, WITH USER_MODE
Error handling             not swallowed
Tests                      with assertions, and a bulk case
Talking while thinking     silence is hard to assess
```

**Narrating is the skill people underuse.** "I'm collecting the asset Ids first so I can query once — if I queried inside the loop this would die at 101 records" tells the interviewer what they need to know even if the code has a bug.

**For a take-home**, the tests are what get read most carefully. A solution with meaningful assertions and a bulk test is a stronger submission than a slightly cleaner one without.

## The design round

For senior and architect roles.

```
"Design the claims platform for a manufacturer with 8 million claims,
 3 million assets, an SAP integration, and a partner portal for engineers."
```

**Structure the answer:**

**Clarify.** Volume, growth, users, existing systems, constraints.

**Data model.** Objects, relationships, and the reason for each master-detail versus lookup choice.

**Sharing.** OWD, and how access is granted — with the sharing-row consequence acknowledged.

**Automation.** Which mechanism where, and why.

**Integration.** Pattern, direction, idempotency, error handling.

**Scale.** Skew, archiving, query selectivity.

**Operations.** Monitoring, deployment, environments.

**The things senior candidates raise unprompted:**

```
"Master-detail on the claim means 8 million records generate no sharing rows,
 which matters at that volume."

"I'd want a retention policy from day one — retrofitting archiving onto a
 populated object is a project."

"External IDs on every integrated object, so the integration is idempotent
 and a retry doesn't duplicate."

"What's the distribution? 8 million claims evenly spread is fine. 8 million
 with 60% under one account is a different design."
```

**Distribution, retention, and idempotency are the three that mark experience**, because they are the ones learned by having got them wrong.

## Behavioural questions

```
"Tell me about a production incident you handled."
"Describe a technical decision you'd make differently now."
"How do you handle a requirement you think is wrong?"
"Tell me about disagreeing with a colleague on a design."
```

**Specific beats impressive.** "We had duplicate payments on 3% of approved claims because the handler didn't compare against oldMap — it ran for eighteen months before finance noticed" is a better answer than a general statement about code quality.

**Include what you got wrong.** A candidate who has never made a mistake has either not built much or is not being candid, and both read poorly.

**Have three stories ready:** an incident, a design decision, and a disagreement. Most behavioural questions map onto one of them.

## Preparing

**Two weeks:**

```
Order of execution, until you can explain the implications
Governor limits, and why they exist
Trigger patterns — write one from memory
Flow versus Apex, with the reasons
Sharing and security in Apex
Three stories, rehearsed
```

**Two months:**

```
The above, plus
Async — every mechanism and when to choose it
Integration patterns and authentication
LWC fundamentals
SOQL selectivity and the query plan
Two full design scenarios, practised aloud
```

**Longer:**

```
Build something. A real org with a real integration teaches more than
any amount of reading, and it produces the specific stories that
behavioural rounds want.
```

**Practising aloud matters more than people expect.** Knowing an answer and articulating it under time pressure are different skills, and only one of them is being tested.

## What weakens an otherwise strong candidate

**Answering before understanding.** Designing a solution to a requirement you have not clarified.

**Absolutism.** "Always use Apex", "never use flows". Every real answer is conditional.

**Bluffing.** "I haven't used Data Cloud, but based on what I know about identity resolution I'd expect..." is a strong answer. Inventing detail is not, and interviewers can tell.

**No questions at the end.** It reads as disinterest, and it wastes the one part of the interview you control.

**Vagueness about your own contribution.** "We built it" invites "what did you do".

## Questions worth asking

```
"How do you decide between flow and Apex?"
"What does your deployment process look like?"
"How large is the org — records, users, integrations?"
"What's the biggest piece of technical debt?"
"How does the team split work between admins and developers?"
"What would my first three months look like?"
```

**The technical debt question is the most informative.** The answer tells you what you would actually be doing, and how candid the team is.
